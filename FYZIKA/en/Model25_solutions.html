1. Atomic Nucleus

1.1 Structure of the nucleus and nuclear forces

The nucleus contains almost all the mass of the atom, yet its size is only of the order of 10⁻¹⁵ m (atom 10⁻¹⁰ m). It consists of nucleons – positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons. A nucleus is written AZX, where Z is the proton number (determines the element), N the neutron number and A = Z + N the nucleon number. Nuclei with the same Z and different N are isotopes (e.g. ¹²C and ¹⁴C).

Protons repel each other electrically, so the nucleus is held together by the nuclear (strong) force:

  • it is attractive and much stronger than the electrostatic repulsion,
  • it has a very short range (about 10⁻¹⁵ m) – it acts only between neighbouring nucleons (saturation),
  • it does not depend on charge – it acts equally between protons and neutrons.

1.2 Mass defect and binding energy

The mass of a nucleus is smaller than the sum of the masses of the free nucleons. The difference is the mass defect:

Δm = Z mp + N mn − mj

Binding energy is the energy released when a nucleus is formed from nucleons – and which must be supplied to break it apart. According to Einstein’s relation:

Ev = Δm c²

A measure of stability is the binding energy per nucleon Ev/A. It is greatest for nuclei around iron (about 8.8 MeV). That is why energy is released both by fission of heavy nuclei and by fusion of light nuclei.

1.3 Natural and artificial radioactivity

Natural radioactivity (H. Becquerel 1896, M. and P. Curie) is the spontaneous transformation of unstable nuclei accompanied by emission of radiation:

  • α – helium nuclei ⁴₂He, low penetration,
  • β⁻ – electrons (a neutron changes into a proton), β⁺ – positrons,
  • γ – high-energy electromagnetic radiation, high penetration.

Decay is random; for a large number of nuclei the law of radioactive decay N = N₀ · 2−t/T applies, where T is the half-life. Activity is measured in becquerels (Bq = 1 decay per second).

Artificial radioactivity (I. and F. Joliot-Curie 1934) arises by bombarding stable nuclei with particles (neutrons in a reactor, accelerators). This is how radionuclides that do not occur in nature are produced.

1.4 Fission chain reaction

A ²³⁵U nucleus that captures a slow neutron splits into two lighter fragments, releasing 2–3 neutrons and about 200 MeV of energy, e.g.:

²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3 n + energy

The released neutrons can split further nuclei – a chain reaction develops. The deciding quantity is the multiplication factor k (the number of neutrons from one fission that cause another fission). For k = 1 the reaction is controlled (nuclear reactor – the moderator slows the neutrons, control rods absorb them); for k > 1 it is uncontrolled (nuclear bomb). A chain reaction is possible only above the critical mass of the fissile material.

1.5 Applications of radionuclides and radiation protection

  • Medicine: radiotherapy of tumours (⁶⁰Co, accelerators), diagnostics (⁹⁹ᵐTc, PET with ¹⁸F), sterilisation of instruments.
  • Technology: testing of welds, thickness and level gauges, tracers (detecting leaks), smoke detectors.
  • Science: radiocarbon dating (¹⁴C), dating of rocks.
  • Energy: nuclear power stations.

Ionising radiation damages cells. Its effect on the body is expressed by the absorbed dose (gray, Gy) and the equivalent dose (sievert, Sv). Protection: time (stay as short as possible), distance (intensity falls with the square of the distance) and shielding. Workers wear personal dosimeters, workplaces are monitored, and radioactive substances are registered and stored in shielded containers. Inhaling or swallowing α emitters is especially dangerous.

2. Electric Current in Electrolytes – Nickel Plating

2.1 Theory

In an electrolyte (a solution or melt of acids, bases and salts) the charge carriers are ions formed by electrolytic dissociation. Cations move to the cathode (−), anions to the anode (+). Substances are deposited on the electrodes – electrolysis.

Faraday’s first law: the mass of the substance deposited is proportional to the charge transferred, m = A Q = A I t. Faraday’s second law: the electrochemical equivalent A = M/(zF). Together:

m = M I t / (z F)

Electroplating: the object is connected as the cathode, the anode is made of the plating metal (nickel), and the electrolyte is a solution of its salt (NiSO₄). Nickel is deposited on the cathode: Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ni. The anode dissolves and replenishes the ions in the solution. Nickel plating protects against corrosion and improves appearance.

2.2 Solution of the task

Given: S = 120 cm² = 1.2 · 10⁻² m², t = 5 h = 18 000 s, I = 0.3 A, M = 58.69 · 10⁻³ kg/mol, z = 2, ρ = 8.8 · 10³ kg/m³.

a) Charge transferred and mass of nickel:

Q = I t = 0.3 A · 18 000 s = 5 400 C
m = M Q / (z F) = 58.69 · 10⁻³ · 5 400 / (2 · 96 485) kg ≈ 1.64 · 10⁻³ kg = 1.64 g

The electrochemical equivalent of nickel is A = M/(zF) ≈ 3.04 · 10⁻⁷ kg/C.

b) The nickel layer forms a “plate” of area S and thickness d; its volume is V = S d = m/ρ:

d = m / (ρ S) = 1.64 · 10⁻³ kg / (8 800 kg/m³ · 1.2 · 10⁻² m²) ≈ 1.56 · 10⁻⁵ m ≈ 16 μm

c) t = 10 h = 36 000 s, I = 0.3 A:

Q = 10 800 C   →   m ≈ 3.28 g, d ≈ 31 μm

d) I = 0.6 A, t = 5 h:

Q = 0.6 A · 18 000 s = 10 800 C   →   m ≈ 3.28 g, d ≈ 31 μm

Comparison: in both cases the same charge Q = It = 10 800 C passes through the electrolyte, so the same mass of nickel is deposited – twice as much as in a). According to Faraday’s first law it does not matter whether we double the time or the current; only the product I · t matters. In practice, however, a higher current density gives a poorer layer (coarse-grained, burnt), so plating is preferably done with a smaller current for a longer time.

a) m ≈ 1.64 g; b) d ≈ 16 μm; c) and d) m ≈ 3.28 g, d ≈ 31 μm – same charge, same result.

3. Experiment – Measuring with a Vernier Caliper and Measurement Errors

3.1 Vernier caliper

A vernier caliper has a main millimetre scale and a sliding part with an auxiliary scale – the vernier. A common vernier has 10 divisions over a length of 9 mm, so one vernier division is 0.1 mm shorter than a millimetre. The caliper can measure outside dimensions, inside dimensions (upper jaws) and depth (depth gauge).

Reading: whole millimetres are read on the main scale before the vernier zero. Tenths of a millimetre are given by the number of the vernier division that coincides with a line on the main scale. The precision is 0.1 mm (0.05 mm for a 20-division vernier, 0.02 mm for 50 divisions).

3.2 Measurement errors

  • Gross errors – a mistake in reading or recording. Such a value is excluded.
  • Systematic errors – they appear in the same way on repetition (non-zero instrument offset, inaccurate scale, unsuitable method). They are removed by checking and correcting the instrument.
  • Random errors – they vary unpredictably (unevenness of the object, unequal pressure of the jaws). They are reduced by repeating the measurement.

For n repeated measurements the best estimate is the arithmetic mean x̄. As an estimate of the absolute error Δx we use the mean deviation (the mean of |xi − x̄|). It must not be smaller than the precision of the instrument. Relative error δx = Δx/x̄. The result is written in the form

x = (x̄ ± Δx) unit,    δx = … %

For a product and quotient the relative errors add; for a power the relative error is multiplied by the exponent.

3.3 Procedure

  1. Check the zero of the caliper (with the jaws closed the vernier zero coincides with the scale zero).
  2. Measure the diameter d of the cylinder 5 times at different places and in different directions, and the height h also 5 times.
  3. Calculate the means, deviations, absolute and relative errors.
  4. Calculate the volume V = π d² h / 4 and its error.
  5. Weigh the cylinder and calculate the density ρ = m/V.

The mean deviation of 0.044 mm is smaller than the precision of the caliper, so we take Δd = Δh = 0.05 mm:

d = (19.98 ± 0.05) mm,   δd = 0.05/19.98 ≈ 0.25 %
h = (40.08 ± 0.05) mm,   δh = 0.05/40.08 ≈ 0.12 %
V = π d² h / 4 = π · 19.98² · 40.08 / 4 mm³ ≈ 12 570 mm³ = 12.57 cm³
δV = 2 δd + δh ≈ 2 · 0.25 % + 0.12 % ≈ 0.62 %   →   ΔV ≈ 0.08 cm³
V = (12.57 ± 0.08) cm³

The diameter appears squared, so its error affects the volume twice – the diameter must be measured most carefully.

Extension – density: mass of the cylinder m = (34.0 ± 0.1) g, δm ≈ 0.3 %.

ρ = m / V = 34.0 g / 12.57 cm³ ≈ 2.71 g/cm³,   δρ = δm + δV ≈ 0.9 %   →   ρ = (2.71 ± 0.03) g/cm³

The table density of aluminium is 2.70 g/cm³ – the cylinder is probably made of aluminium.
