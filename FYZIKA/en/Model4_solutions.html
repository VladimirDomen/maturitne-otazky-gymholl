1. Kinematics of a Particle

Basic concepts

Mechanical motion is the change of position of a body with respect to a chosen frame of reference over time. A particle (point mass) is a model of a body whose dimensions can be neglected for the motion being studied. A frame of reference consists of a reference body, a coordinate system and a clock.

Relativity of motion and rest: A passenger sitting in a train is at rest relative to the train and in motion relative to the platform. Motion cannot be described without specifying a frame of reference.

The trajectory is the geometric line traced out by the moving point; the distance travelled s is the length of the part of the trajectory covered. Velocity expresses the rate of change of position with time, acceleration the rate of change of velocity with time.

By trajectory we distinguish rectilinear and curvilinear motion; by velocity uniform and non-uniform motion (for example uniformly accelerated or decelerated).

Calculation – uniform braking

v₀ = 72 km/h; a = −1.8 m/s²; final velocity v = 0. Determine the stopping time and the braking distance.

1. Converting the velocity:

v₀ = 72 : 3.6 = 20 m/s

2. Stopping time: for constant acceleration v = v₀ + at, so

t = (0 − 20)/(−1.8) ≈ 11.11 s

3. Braking distance:

s = v₀t + ½at² = 20·11.11 − 0.9·11.11² ≈ 111.1 m

Check using the equation without time:

v² = v₀² + 2as ⇒ s = (0 − 20²)/(2·(−1.8)) ≈ 111.1 m
Result: The car stops in approximately 11.1 s and travels about 111 m.

2. Stationary Magnetic Field

Theory

A stationary magnetic field is a magnetic field that does not change with time. It arises, for example, around a wire carrying a steady electric current. The magnetic flux density B is a vector quantity; its unit is the tesla (T). The magnetic field lines around a long straight wire are concentric circles. Their orientation is given by the right-hand grip rule: the thumb points in the direction of the conventional current, the curled fingers in the direction of the magnetic field lines.

B = μ₀I / (2πr),   μ₀ = 4π · 10⁻⁷ T·m/A

Two parallel wires with currents in the same direction attract each other; with opposite directions they repel. The principle of superposition applies to the magnetic field: the resultant flux density is the vector sum of the flux densities of the individual wires.

Problem – magnetic flux density at the midpoint

1. Distance of point P from each wire:

r = d/2 = 0.15/2 = 0.075 m

2. Flux density due to the first wire:

B₁ = μ₀·12/(2π·0.075) = 3.2·10⁻⁵ T = 32 μT

3. Flux density due to the second wire:

B₂ = μ₀·10/(2π·0.075) ≈ 2.67·10⁻⁵ T ≈ 26.7 μT

4. Directions of the vectors: If both currents flow to the right, at point P the field of the upper wire points into the plane of the diagram and the field of the lower wire out of the plane of the diagram. The fields therefore have opposite directions. Since B₁ > B₂, the resultant field points into the plane of the diagram.

B = |B₁ − B₂| = 5.33·10⁻⁶ T ≈ 5.3 μT
Result: B ≈ 5.3 μT; for the current directions shown, the resultant flux density points into the plane of the diagram.

3. The Microworld – Radioactive Decay

Theory

An atom consists of a nucleus with protons and neutrons and an electron shell. The number of protons is the proton (atomic) number Z, the number of nucleons is the nucleon (mass) number A. Radioactivity is the spontaneous transformation of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by the emission of particles or electromagnetic radiation.

  • α radiation: helium nuclei (2 protons and 2 neutrons); low penetrating power.
  • β radiation: electrons or positrons produced in nuclear transformations.
  • γ radiation: high-energy electromagnetic radiation.

The decay of an individual nucleus is a random event, but for a large number of nuclei the change in their number can be described by an exponential law.

Law of radioactive decay

N(t) = N₀ e−λt = N₀·2−t/T½

N(t) is the number of undecayed nuclei at time t, N₀ the initial number, λ the decay constant and T½ the half-life. T½ = ln 2 / λ.

Graph of the number of undecayed nuclei against time

At the start N(0) = N₀. After one half-life N₀/2 remains, after two N₀/4, after three N₀/8. It is a decreasing exponential curve that approaches zero but, in the mathematical model, never reaches it in a finite time.

N(nT½) = N₀ / 2ⁿ
Conclusion: After each half-life, the number of remaining radioactive nuclei is halved.
