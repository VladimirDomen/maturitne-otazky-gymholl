1. Structure and Properties of Liquids

Structure and surface layer

The particles of a liquid are close together, interact through attractive and repulsive forces and move randomly. A liquid has an approximately constant volume but takes the shape of its container. Inside the liquid, the action of the surrounding particles balances out on average. At a free surface the surroundings of a particle are different, and the surface layer has additional energy compared with the interior.

To enlarge the surface, work must be done. A liquid of given volume therefore tends towards the smallest possible surface; a small free drop is approximately spherical.

ΔEₚ = σΔS; for constant σ: Eₚ = σS

σ is the surface tension, S the surface area. The equation expresses the surface energy at constant temperature and composition.

Surface force and surface tension

The surface force acts in the plane of the surface, perpendicular to its edge, and tends to reduce the surface area.

σ = F/l; F = σl
[σ] = N/m = J/m²

l is the length of the edge of one surface. A soap film has two surfaces, so a force F = 2σl acts on a movable bar of length l. Surface tension depends on the liquid, the temperature and impurities; it generally decreases as the temperature rises.

Wetting and meniscus

The decisive factors are the interactions between the liquid, the solid and the surrounding gas. The contact angle θ is measured in the liquid between the surface of the solid and the tangent to the liquid surface.

PhenomenonContact angleExample and meniscus
Wettingθ < 90°Water on clean glass; concave meniscus
Non-wettingθ > 90°Mercury on glass; convex meniscus

Capillary phenomena

In a narrow vertical tube the liquid level can rise above the level of the surrounding liquid (capillary rise) or fall below it (capillary depression).

The vertical component of the surface force acting around the circumference of the capillary is 2πrσ cos θ. In equilibrium it is balanced by the weight of the liquid column:

2πrσ cos θ = ρgπr²h
h = 2σ cos θ/(ρgr)

r is the inner radius of the tube and ρ the density of the liquid; we neglect the density of air. For wetting h is positive, for non-wetting negative. The narrower the capillary, the greater the magnitude of the change in level. Examples: soaking of paper, wicks and pores in building materials.

Calculation of the volume of mercury

V₀ = 50 cm³, t₀ = 18 °C, t = 39 °C. We use the approximate table value β = 1.82 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, suitable around room temperature. In this range we consider β constant.

Δt = 39 − 18 = 21 °C = 21 K (temperature difference)
V = V₀(1 + βΔt)
V = 50[1 + (1.82 × 10⁻⁴) × 21] cm³
V = 50 × 1.003822 cm³ = 50.1911 cm³
At 39 °C the mercury has a volume of approximately 50.19 cm³.
The increase in volume is ΔV ≈ 0.191 cm³.

This is the expansion of the mercury itself. The expansion of the container is not part of the calculation.

The table value of the expansion coefficient of mercury is rounded to 1.82 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. Reference for the temperature dependence: data on the density and expansion of mercury (NIST).

2. Interference in an Oil Film

Refractive indices

nₒ = c/vₒ = (3 × 10⁸)/(2 × 10⁸) = 1.50
nw = c/vw = (3 × 10⁸)/(2.25 × 10⁸) ≈ 1.33

nair < nₒ and nₒ > nw. The two reflected waves arise by splitting the same incident wave and can interfere.

The drawing separates the paths slightly obliquely; the calculation below applies to normal incidence.

  • Reflection at the air → oil boundary: reflection from an optically denser medium, the phase changes by π (half a period).
  • Reflection at the oil → water boundary: reflection from an optically less dense medium, no such phase shift occurs.

The second wave passes through the film down and back, i.e. a geometric path of 2d. The optical path difference is 2nₒd. In addition, there is a phase difference of π between the waves due to the first reflection.

2nₒd = 2 × 1.5 × 0.2 μm = 0.6 μm = 600 nm
Δφ = 2π · (2nₒd/λ₀) + π

λ₀ denotes the wavelength in a vacuum, approximately also in air. In oil λₒ = λ₀/nₒ. The frequency does not change when passing between media.

Reinforcement of the reflected light

Since only one reflection changes the phase by π, the reflection maximum occurs when the optical path difference contains an odd number of half-wavelengths:

2nₒd = (m + ½)λ₀, m = 0, 1, 2, …
λ₀ = 600/(m + ½) nm = 1200/(2m + 1) nm

Weakening does not mean complete extinction: the amplitudes of the two reflected waves are generally not equal. We use the school model of two rays and constant refractive indices; dispersion is neglected. The speed in water mainly determines whether the second reflection changes the phase.

3. Current–Voltage Characteristic of a Diode

Principle

A semiconductor diode contains a PN junction. In the forward direction we connect the positive terminal of the source to the anode and the negative terminal to the cathode. The barrier of the junction decreases and the current increases. In the reverse direction the barrier increases and only a small reverse current flows until breakdown occurs.

Equipment and circuit

An adjustable DC source, a diode, a protective resistor, an ammeter, a voltmeter and leads.

The protective resistor limits the current. Its value is chosen according to the source voltage and the permitted diode current: R ≥ (Usource,max − UD)/Imax. For example, with a source up to 5 V and an ordinary silicon diode, 1 kΩ can be used for a school measurement if the diode parameters allow a current of the order of a few mA.

Procedure

  1. Set the source to zero voltage and assemble the circuit. Check the polarity and the ranges of the meters.
  2. Connect the diode in the forward direction. Gradually increase the source voltage and record the voltage directly across the diode and the current. In the region of steep current rise choose smaller steps.
  3. Do not exceed the permitted current and power of the diode. Reduce the voltage to zero before changing the connection.
  4. Reverse the diode and measure the reverse direction only up to the voltage permitted for the given type. An ordinary diode is not driven into breakdown in this measurement.
  5. For the reverse direction, use a more sensitive current range if possible. If the meter reads zero, the reverse current may simply be smaller than its resolution.
  6. Plot the graph I = f(U). Draw the forward direction in the first quadrant and the reverse direction in the third; if the current units differ, use separate graphs.

Evaluation

The characteristic of a diode is non-linear: the ratio U/I is not constant. For an ordinary silicon diode, a significant forward current is often observed at around 0.6–0.7 V, but this is not an exact, fixed threshold; it depends on the current, the temperature and the type of diode. In the reverse direction the current at the permitted voltage is very small.

The diode conducts current much better in the forward than in the reverse direction. This property enables the rectification of alternating current.
