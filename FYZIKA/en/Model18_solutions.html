1. Free Oscillations

1.1 Oscillatory motion and its quantities

Oscillatory motion is a periodic motion of a body about its equilibrium position. A body that oscillates is called an oscillator – e.g. a mass on a spring or a pendulum.

  • Period T – time of one oscillation [s].
  • Frequency f – number of oscillations per second, f = 1/T [Hz].
  • Angular frequency ω = 2πf = 2π/T [rad·s⁻¹].
  • Phase φ = ωt + φ₀ [rad] determines the instantaneous state of the oscillation. The initial phase φ₀ is the phase at time t = 0.

1.2 Instantaneous displacement and amplitude

The instantaneous displacement y is the instantaneous distance of the oscillating body from the equilibrium position, with a sign according to the side. The amplitude of displacement A is the greatest value of the displacement. For undamped oscillations it is constant.

1.3 Kinematic equation

Simple harmonic motion is the projection of uniform circular motion with radius A onto a diameter of the circle. Hence:

y = A sin(ωt + φ₀)

Velocity and acceleration:

v = ωA cos(ωt + φ₀)     a = −ω²A sin(ωt + φ₀) = −ω² y

The acceleration is proportional to the displacement and always points towards the equilibrium position. At the equilibrium position the velocity is maximum and the acceleration is zero. At the extreme positions the velocity is zero and the acceleration is maximum.

Free oscillations occur when the oscillator is displaced and left to oscillate without any further external influence. It oscillates at its natural frequency, which depends only on the properties of the oscillator. For a mass–spring oscillator:

T₀ = 2π √(m/k)     f₀ = 1/(2π) · √(k/m)

Real free oscillations are damped by resistive forces – the amplitude gradually decreases. Ideal undamped oscillations have a constant amplitude.

1.4 Solution of the task

a) The greatest displacement in the graph is 90 mm:

b) The graph starts to repeat at 0.2 s – one complete oscillation takes this time (e.g. between 0 s and 0.2 s or between two neighbouring maxima):

c) At time t = 0 the displacement is zero and the body moves in the positive direction (the graph rises). This corresponds to sin φ₀ = 0 with an increasing sine:

2. Motion in a Gravitational Field – Horizontal Projection

2.1 Motion in a uniform gravitational field

Near the Earth’s surface the gravitational field is considered uniform: a constant weight mg acts on the body and (without air resistance) the body has a constant acceleration g ≈ 9.81 m/s² downwards. The type of motion depends on the initial velocity:

  • free fall – v₀ = 0, uniformly accelerated motion, s = ½gt², v = gt,
  • vertical upward projection – v₀ points upwards, the motion is first decelerated, then free fall,
  • horizontal projection – v₀ is horizontal,
  • oblique projection – v₀ makes an angle α with the horizontal, the trajectory is a parabola.

2.2 Horizontal projection as a combined motion

According to the principle of independence of motions we combine two motions:

  • in the horizontal direction uniform motion with speed v₀ (no force acts horizontally),
  • in the vertical direction free fall.

With the origin on the ground below the point of projection:

x = v₀ t     y = h − ½ g t²
vₓ = v₀     vᵧ = g t (downwards)     v = √(v₀² + g²t²)

The trajectory is part of a parabola. The time of fall does not depend on v₀ – the body falls for as long as in free fall from the same height:

td = √(2h/g)     d = v₀ td = v₀ √(2h/g)

2.3 Solution of the task

Given: v₀ = 60 m/s, h = 60 m, g = 10 m/s².

a) Position at time t = 2 s:

x = v₀ t = 60 m/s · 2 s = 120 m
drop: ½ g t² = ½ · 10 · 2² m = 20 m   →   y = 60 m − 20 m = 40 m

The body is 120 m horizontally from the point of projection and 40 m above the ground.

b) Velocity at time t = 2 s:

vₓ = 60 m/s     vᵧ = g t = 10 · 2 m/s = 20 m/s
v = √(60² + 20²) m/s = √4 000 m/s ≈ 63.2 m/s

The velocity makes an angle β with the horizontal, where tan β = vᵧ/vₓ = 20/60, so β ≈ 18.4° downwards.

c) Time of impact (y = 0):

td = √(2h/g) = √(2 · 60 / 10) s = √12 s ≈ 3.46 s

d) Range:

d = v₀ td = 60 m/s · 3.46 s ≈ 208 m

At impact vᵧ = g td ≈ 34.6 m/s, the speed is v = √(60² + 34.6²) m/s ≈ 69.3 m/s and tan β = 34.6/60 ≈ 0.577, so the body lands at an angle of 30°. The law of conservation of energy gives the same speed: v = √(v₀² + 2gh) = √(3 600 + 1 200) m/s ≈ 69.3 m/s.

t = 2 s: x = 120 m, height 40 m, v ≈ 63 m/s; impact after td ≈ 3.5 s at a distance d ≈ 208 m.

With g = 9.81 m/s²: the drop in 2 s is 19.6 m, v ≈ 63.1 m/s, td ≈ 3.50 s and d ≈ 210 m.

3. Experiment – Diameter of an Oleic Acid Molecule

3.1 Principle

Oleic acid does not dissolve in water and spreads out on the water surface. Its molecules have a polar –COOH group at one end, which binds to water, and a long non-polar hydrocarbon chain, which “stands away” from the water. A small amount of the acid therefore spreads into a monomolecular layer. Its thickness d corresponds to the size of one molecule.

If we know the volume V of acid in a drop and the area S of the film, the thickness of the layer follows from the volume of a “cylinder”:

V = S · d    →    d = V/S,    S = π D²/4

A drop of pure acid would form a film several metres across. We therefore use a dilute solution of oleic acid in alcohol. The alcohol dissolves in the water and evaporates, leaving only the acid on the surface.

3.2 Apparatus

A wide shallow tray (at least 30 cm), water, fine powder to sprinkle on the surface (talc, lycopodium or baby powder), solution of oleic acid in alcohol of known concentration (e.g. 1 : 1 000), dropper or syringe, measuring cylinder, ruler, transparent sheet with a square grid.

3.3 Procedure

  1. Volume of a drop: let drops fall into a measuring cylinder until a volume can be read (e.g. 1 cm³) and count the drops. The volume of one drop is Vk = V/n.
  2. Pour water into a clean tray, let the surface settle and sprinkle it evenly with a thin layer of powder.
  3. From a small height let one drop of the solution fall into the middle. The acid pushes the powder aside and forms a film.
  4. When the film has settled, measure its diameter in several directions and take the average. If the shape is irregular, trace it onto the grid sheet and find the area by counting squares.
  5. Repeat the experiment with clean water and fresh powder.

3.4 Sample evaluation

The following numbers are model data; they are not a real measurement.

50 drops make 1 cm³, so Vk = 0.02 cm³. The solution has a concentration of 1 : 1 000, so the volume of acid in one drop is:

V = 0.02 cm³ / 1 000 = 2 · 10⁻⁵ cm³ = 2 · 10⁻¹¹ m³

d = V/S = 2 · 10⁻¹¹ m³ / 1.34 · 10⁻² m² ≈ 1.5 · 10⁻⁹ m = 1.5 nm

The result shows that molecules are of the order of 10⁻⁹ m. The length of an oleic acid molecule is about 2 nm. The method gives the correct order of magnitude, not an exact value.

3.5 Assumptions and sources of error

  • The layer is exactly one molecule thick and continuous, without gaps and without overlapping molecules.
  • The molecules stand perpendicular to the surface – in reality they are tilted and d is the height of the layer rather than the length of the molecule.
  • The largest errors come from determining the drop volume and the concentration of the solution and from the irregular shape of the film.
  • Impurities on the surface (grease from fingers) reduce the area of the film. The tray must be thoroughly clean.
Conclusion: From d = V/S the thickness of the monomolecular layer is d ≈ 1.5 · 10⁻⁹ m – the diameter (length) of an oleic acid molecule is of the order of nanometres.
