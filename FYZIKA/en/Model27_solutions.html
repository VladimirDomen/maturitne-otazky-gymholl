1. Electromagnetic Waves

1.1 Production

Electromagnetic waves are produced by accelerated electric charges – for example by electrons oscillating in an antenna. A time-varying electric field creates a varying magnetic field, which in turn creates an electric field. The field thus spreads through space even without a material medium.

The source can be an LC oscillating circuit. In a closed circuit the energy is only exchanged between the capacitor and the coil. If the circuit is “opened out” into an antenna (an electric dipole), it radiates electromagnetic waves with frequency f = 1/(2π√(LC)).

Electromagnetic waves are transverse: the electric field strength vector 𝐄 and the magnetic flux density vector 𝐁 are perpendicular to each other and both perpendicular to the direction of propagation. They oscillate in phase.

1.2 Types of electromagnetic waves

All types have the same nature and the same speed in a vacuum. They differ in wavelength, frequency and photon energy E = hf = hc/λ, and therefore in how they are produced and in their effects.

TypeWavelength (approximately)Source, use

Going down the table the wavelength decreases and the frequency and photon energy increase. Ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma radiation can damage living tissue.

1.3 Speed of electromagnetic waves

In a vacuum every electromagnetic wave travels at the speed of light:

c = 1/√(ε₀μ₀) ≈ 3 · 10⁸ m/s

In a material medium the speed is smaller, v = c/n, where n is the refractive index of the medium. When passing into another medium the frequency does not change – it is set by the source. The speed changes, and therefore so does the wavelength: λ = v/f = λ₀/n. The refractive index depends on frequency (dispersion), so different colours are refracted differently.

Unlike mechanical waves, electromagnetic waves do not need a material medium and are fastest in a vacuum. Mechanical waves do not travel in a vacuum at all.

1.4 Solution of the task

We find the frequency from the data in the vacuum; it does not change on passing into the medium:

f = c/λ₀ = (3 · 10⁸ m/s)/(240 · 10⁻⁹ m) = 1.25 · 10¹⁵ Hz

Wavelength in the medium:

λ = v/f = (2 · 10⁸ m/s)/(1.25 · 10¹⁵ Hz) = 1.6 · 10⁻⁷ m = 160 nm

The refractive index of the medium is n = c/v = 1.5, so λ = λ₀/n = 240 nm / 1.5 = 160 nm. Energy of one photon:

E = hf = 6.63 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s · 1.25 · 10¹⁵ Hz ≈ 8.3 · 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 5.2 eV
f = 1.25 · 10¹⁵ Hz, λ = 160 nm. It is ultraviolet radiation.

The type of radiation is determined by the wavelength in a vacuum (or by the frequency), not by the wavelength in a material. Radiation with λ₀ = 240 nm belongs to the UVC range, which is absorbed by the ozone layer and is used for disinfection.

2. Structure and Properties of Solids

2.1 Linear thermal expansion

The particles of a solid oscillate about their equilibrium positions. As the temperature rises, the amplitude of the oscillations and the mean distances between the particles increase, so the body gets longer. For temperature changes that are not too large:

Δl = αl₀Δt     l = l₀(1 + αΔt)

α is the coefficient of linear thermal expansion [K⁻¹]. Expansion must be allowed for in rails, bridges and pipelines (expansion joints, compensators). It is used in a bimetallic strip.

2.2 Tensile deformation and Hooke’s law

If a force F acts perpendicular to the cross-section S of a rod, a normal stress arises in it and the rod extends. The strain is the ratio of the extension to the original length:

σn = F/S     ε = Δl/l₀

For elastic deformation Hooke’s law applies: the normal stress is directly proportional to the strain.

σn = Eε

E is Young’s modulus [Pa]. Hooke’s law holds only up to the limit of proportionality; at larger stress a permanent deformation occurs and at the ultimate tensile strength the material breaks.

2.3 Solution of the task

a) Extension when heated by Δt = 1 °C:

Δl = αl₀Δt = 1.2 · 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ · 1.5 m · 1 K = 1.8 · 10⁻⁵ m = 0.018 mm

b) The same extension means the same strain ε = Δl/l₀ = αΔt = 1.2 · 10⁻⁵. From Hooke’s law and the cross-section S = πd²/4:

S = π · (0.020 m)²/4 ≈ 3.14 · 10⁻⁴ m²
F = σnS = EεS = 2.1 · 10¹¹ Pa · 1.2 · 10⁻⁵ · 3.14 · 10⁻⁴ m² ≈ 790 N

c) Normal stress:

σn = Eε = 2.1 · 10¹¹ Pa · 1.2 · 10⁻⁵ = 2.52 · 10⁶ Pa ≈ 2.5 MPa
a) Δl = 0.018 mm; b) F ≈ 790 N; c) σn ≈ 2.5 MPa.

Although the extension is only 18 μm, it needs a force of about 790 N. The rod would push on fixed obstacles with the same force if it were prevented from extending when heated by 1 °C. The force does not depend on the length of the rod – only on E, α, Δt and the cross-section.

3. Experiment – Outflow Speed of a Liquid

3.1 Theory

A jet of water leaving a hole horizontally at a height h above the base moves like a body in horizontal projection. Horizontally it moves uniformly, vertically it falls freely:

x = v₀t     h = ½gt²

From the second equation t = √(2h/g). Substituting into the first gives the outflow speed from the measured range x:

v₀ = x √(g/(2h))

The theoretical outflow speed of an ideal liquid is given by Torricelli’s formula, which follows from Bernoulli’s equation. H is the depth of the hole below the surface:

v = √(2gH)

3.2 Equipment

A tall plastic container or bottle with a small hole in the side (diameter 2–4 mm), water, a measuring tape, a base or tray to catch the jet, a marker.

3.3 Procedure

  1. Place the container on the table so that the hole points horizontally. Measure the height h of the hole above the base on which the jet lands.
  2. Fill the container and mark the water level. Measure the depth H of the hole below the surface.
  3. Open the hole and measure the horizontal distance x from the wall below the hole to the point where the jet lands. The level falls, so measure quickly or top up the water to keep the level constant.
  4. Repeat the measurement for several depths H.
  5. For each measurement calculate v₀ from the range and compare it with v = √(2gH).

3.4 Sample evaluation

The following numbers are model data for h = 0.50 m and g = 9.81 m/s²; they are not a real measurement.

Conclusion: The outflow speed found from horizontal projection is about 5 % smaller than the ideal Torricelli speed. As the depth of the hole below the surface increases, both the outflow speed and the range increase.

3.5 Uncertainties

A real liquid has internal friction and the jet narrows just after the hole, so the real speed is smaller than the ideal one. Measurement errors are caused by the falling water level, an inaccurately determined landing point (the jet splashes), a hole that does not point exactly horizontally, and air resistance. Repeating the measurement and using a thin wall with a smooth hole helps.
