1. Electron Shell of the Atom

Electrons in an atom occupy quantum states with certain allowed energies. The energy of a bound atom cannot take any value: when an electron passes between levels, it absorbs or emits a photon whose energy equals the difference between the energies of these levels.

Eph = hf = |E2 − E1|

An emission spectrum arises when atoms emit photons as they pass from higher energy states to lower ones. An absorption spectrum arises when atoms absorb photons with the energy needed to reach a higher state. A rarefied atomic gas produces a line spectrum because the energies of the allowed transitions take only certain values. Examples: a glowing discharge lamp (emission), dark absorption lines in the solar spectrum (absorption).

Calculation

Eph = 6.626 · 10−34 · 4.57 · 1014 J ≈ 3.028 · 10−19 J
Eph ≈ (3.028 · 10−19) / (1.602 · 10−19) eV ≈ 1.89 eV
The magnitude of the change in the energy of the atom is |ΔE| ≈ 3.03 · 10−19 J ≈ 1.89 eV. For emission ΔEatom = −Eph, for absorption ΔEatom = +Eph.

2. Electric Current

The electrical resistance of a metal is due to the scattering of conduction electrons by the vibrating ions of the crystal lattice and by lattice defects. For many metals and alloys, an approximately linear dependence of resistance on temperature can be used within a limited temperature range.

a) Cross-sectional area of the wire

S = πd² / 4 = π · (1.2 · 10−3)² / 4 ≈ 1.131 · 10−6 m²

b) Resistance at 20 °C

R20 = ρl/S = (4 · 10−7 · 1.5) / (1.131 · 10−6) ≈ 0.5305 Ω

c) Current at 20 °C – including the internal resistance of the source

I20 = Ue / (R20 + Ri) = 12 / (0.5305 + 0.2) ≈ 16.43 A

d) Resistance at 90 °C

ΔT = 90 − 20 = 70 K
R90 = R20(1 + αΔT) = 0.5305 · (1 + 11 · 10−5 · 70) ≈ 0.5346 Ω

e) Current at 90 °C

I90 = Ue / (R90 + Ri) = 12 / (0.5346 + 0.2) ≈ 16.34 A
At 20 °C the current is approximately 16.43 A; after heating to 90 °C it decreases to approximately 16.34 A.
The cross-section is calculated from the diameter of the wire itself, not from the diameter of the coiled spring. As stated in the problem, the cross-section and length of the wire are considered constant during heating. The 12 V is the electromotive force of the source; the internal resistance Ri belongs in the denominator.

3. Structure and Properties of Gases – Experiment

Suggested equipment and procedure

A large transparent syringe without a needle, a tight stopper or a finger to close the outlet; ideally a temperature sensor with a small thermal inertia. We trap air in the syringe. We push the piston in quickly, hold it for a moment and then let the gas expand rapidly by pulling the piston out.

Observation: During rapid compression the temperature of the air rises; during rapid expansion, in which the gas does work on the piston, it falls. Without a sensor, the change in temperature in a small syringe may not be reliably observable directly – the demonstration is mainly qualitative.

Explanation: The first law of thermodynamics, in the convention where W is the work done on the gas, reads:

ΔU = Q + Won gas

In a sufficiently rapid process, the heat exchanged with the surroundings is small (Q ≈ 0). During compression positive work is done on the gas, so its internal energy and temperature increase. During expansion the gas does work, and its internal energy and temperature decrease.

Note: An increase in volume alone need not always lead to cooling (for example in the free expansion of an ideal gas into a vacuum). What matters is whether the gas does work during expansion and how much heat it exchanges with the surroundings. Do not use a needle and do not overload the syringe.
