1. Wave Optics

1.1 Light as an electromagnetic wave

Light behaves as a wave: it interferes, diffracts and can be polarised. Polarisation shows that it is a transverse wave. Maxwell’s theory predicted that electromagnetic waves travel in a vacuum at c = 1/√(ε₀μ₀) ≈ 3 · 10⁸ m/s – the same speed as light. Light is therefore an electromagnetic wave with wavelengths of about 380 – 760 nm; the vectors 𝐄 and 𝐁 oscillate perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation. The colour of light is determined by its frequency.

1.2 Coherence and optical path

A steady interference pattern is formed only when coherent waves combine – they have the same frequency and a constant phase difference. Two independent sources (two light bulbs) are not coherent. Coherent waves are obtained by splitting light from one source – for example by reflection at the two surfaces of a thin film.

The optical path is the product of the refractive index and the geometric path, l = n·s. In a material with index n light travels n times more slowly, so a path s in the material corresponds to a path n·s in a vacuum.

Interference conditions for a path difference Δl (without phase jumps on reflection):

maximum: Δl = kλ     minimum: Δl = (2k + 1)λ/2     k = 0, 1, 2, …

The number k is the order of the interference maximum or minimum.

1.3 Interference in a thin film

Ray 1 is reflected from the upper surface of the film, ray 2 passes through the film, is reflected from the lower surface and comes back out. At normal incidence their path difference is 2nd. On reflection from an optically denser medium the phase changes by π, which corresponds to a path of λ/2. If the film is in air (a soap film), the jump occurs only for ray 1:

maximum: 2nd = (2k + 1)λ/2     minimum: 2nd = kλ

With white light, different colours are reinforced for different thicknesses – that is why soap bubbles and oil patches on water are coloured. It is used in anti-reflection coatings on glasses and camera lenses.

1.4 Dispersion of light on refraction

The refractive index of a material depends on the frequency (wavelength) of light – dispersion. In glass the refractive index for violet light is larger than for red, so violet light is refracted more. White light, composed of different colours, is therefore split into a spectrum on refraction at a boundary. At a plane boundary the refracted rays of different colours diverge only slightly; the spectrum becomes clear in a prism, where light is refracted twice in the same sense. In nature a rainbow forms in this way – by refraction and reflection in water droplets.

sin α / sin β = n(λ)     nviolet > nred

2. Electric Current

R = 15 Ω, t = 2 min = 120 s, Q = 30 C, e = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C.

a) Charge is quantised – it is a whole multiple of the elementary charge, Q = Ne:

N = Q/e = 30 C / (1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C) ≈ 1.87 · 10²⁰

b) Electric current is the ordered motion of free charge carriers. As a quantity it gives the charge passing through the cross-section of a conductor per unit time. Its unit is the ampere (A):

I = Q/t = 30 C / 120 s = 0.25 A

c) Electric voltage between two points is the ratio of the work done by electric forces in moving a charge between these points to the magnitude of the charge: U = W/Q. Its unit is the volt (V = J/C). For a resistor Ohm’s law applies, U = RI:

U = RI = 15 Ω · 0.25 A = 3.75 V

The electric forces did work W = QU = 30 C · 3.75 V = 112.5 J, which was converted into heat in the resistor.

d) At the same current I = 0.25 A:

U = RI = 50 Ω · 0.25 A = 12.5 V
a) 1.87 · 10²⁰ electrons; b) I = 0.25 A; c) U = 3.75 V; d) U = 12.5 V.

At the same current the voltage is directly proportional to the resistance. For the same current to flow through a resistor with greater resistance, the source must supply a greater voltage.

3. Experiment – Stationary Magnetic Field

3.1 Principle

A stationary magnetic field does not change with time. It is produced by permanent magnets and by conductors carrying a steady current. Iron filings become magnetised in a magnetic field and behave like small compass needles – they line up along the field lines. Field lines are closed curves; outside a magnet they run from the north pole N to the south pole S. Their direction is found with a compass – its north pole points along the field lines.

3.2 Equipment

Bar magnets, iron filings in a shaker, a transparent board or stiff paper, a compass, a conductor passed vertically through the board, a loop (coil) passed through the board, a low-voltage source with a larger current (several amperes), a switch.

3.3 Procedure and observations

  1. Bar magnet: place the magnet under the board, sprinkle filings and tap gently. The lines come out of the poles and surround the magnet; they are densest at the poles, where the field is strongest.
  2. Straight conductor: the conductor passes perpendicularly through the board. When the current is switched on, the filings form concentric circles around the conductor. The direction of the lines is given by Ampère’s right-hand grip rule: the thumb points along the current, the fingers along the field lines. The field weakens with distance from the conductor.
  3. Loop (coil): there are circles around each side of the loop, and inside the loop the lines are almost parallel. A coil has a field similar to that of a bar magnet; inside a long coil the field is uniform.
  4. Unlike poles (N to S): the lines pass from one magnet into the other – the magnets attract each other.
  5. Like poles (N to N): the lines bend away from each other and between the poles there is a place where the field is almost zero (no filings) – the magnets repel each other.

In the figures ⊙ means a current towards the observer, ⊗ away from the observer. The red dot between the like poles marks the point of zero field.

Conclusion: The magnetic field of both a magnet and a current-carrying conductor has closed field lines. The field of a straight conductor consists of concentric circles; the field of a loop and a coil resembles that of a bar magnet. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel.

3.4 Safety and errors

Use only low voltage and switch the current on briefly – the conductor heats up. Do not sprinkle filings directly onto the magnet (they are hard to remove) and protect your eyes. With a weak current the pattern near the conductor is faint; a larger current or more turns helps.
