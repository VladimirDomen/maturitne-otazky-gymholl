1. Mechanics of Fluids

Properties of liquids and gases

Liquids and gases are fluids: they have no fixed shape of their own and can flow. A liquid has an almost constant volume and is only slightly compressible; in a container it forms a free surface. A gas fills all the available space and is highly compressible.

An ideal liquid is a model of an incompressible liquid without internal friction (viscosity). A real liquid has viscosity and is compressible to some extent.

Hydrostatic pressure

It is caused by the weight of the liquid. For a liquid of constant density:

ph = ρgh
p = p0 + ρgh

ρ is the density of the liquid, g the gravitational acceleration, h the depth below the surface and p0 the pressure at the surface. The pressure is the same at the same depth and does not depend on the shape of the container. The pressure force acts perpendicular to the surface.

Pascal’s law

A change in pressure produced by an external force in an enclosed fluid is transmitted unchanged to every point of the fluid. For an ideal hydraulic device with pistons at the same height:

F₁ / S₁ = F₂ / S₂   ⇒   F₂ = F₁ · S₂ / S₁

The larger piston gives a larger force but moves a shorter distance. The device does not increase the work done. Examples: hydraulic jack, hydraulic press and brakes.

Archimedes’ principle

A body immersed in a fluid experiences a vertical upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the body.

FB = ρfluid gVimmersed part

The buoyant force results from the difference of pressure forces: the lower part of the body is generally under greater pressure than the upper part. The principle also holds for gases.

  • Sinking: the weight is greater than the buoyant force.
  • Floating fully submerged: a fully immersed body has a buoyant force equal to its weight.
  • Floating at the surface: the body settles partly immersed, at the depth where the buoyant force equals its weight.

Sphere in water – forces

Three forces act on the sphere: its weight Fg downwards, the buoyant force FB upwards and the tension of the suspension T upwards. The spring balance reads T = 40.6 N. The sphere acts on the suspension with an opposite force directed downwards.

T + FB = mg
FB = mg − T
V = (mg − T) / (ρwg)
ρsphere = m / V = mρwg / (mg − T)

Variant A: g = 9.81 m/s²

Fg = 4.56 · 9.81 = 44.7336 N
FB = 44.7336 − 40.6 = 4.1336 N
V = 4.1336 / (1000 · 9.81) ≈ 0.00042137 m³
ρsphere ≈ 10 822 kg/m³

Variant B: g = 10 m/s²

Fg = 4.56 · 10 = 45.6 N
FB = 45.6 − 40.6 = 5.0 N
V = 5.0 / (1000 · 10) = 0.0005 m³
ρsphere = 9120 kg/m³

2. Alternating Current and the Transformer

For a sinusoidal alternating current, the magnitude and direction of the current change periodically. The rms (effective) value corresponds to the direct current that would produce the same heating effect in the same resistor.

u(t) = Um sin(ωt),   U = Um / √2
ω = 2πf,   f = 1 / Tperiod

A transformer consists of a primary and a secondary winding on a common magnetic core. Alternating current in the primary winding produces a time-varying magnetic flux, which induces a voltage in the secondary winding. A steady direct current does not produce a varying flux, so an ordinary transformer cannot continuously transform its voltage.

k = N₂ / N₁ = U₂ / U₁
U₂ = 220 · 2000 / 1100 = 400 V

For an ideal transformer with a resistive load, the input and output powers are equal:

P₁ = P₂   ⇒   U₁I₁ = U₂I₂
I₂ = U₁I₁ / U₂ = (220 · 0.2) / 400 = 0.11 A

The secondary voltage is 400 V and the secondary current is 0.11 A. It is a step-up transformer.

Check: P₁ = 220 · 0.2 = 44 W; P₂ = 400 · 0.11 = 44 W. A real transformer has losses, for example due to heating of the windings and the core.

3. Focal Length for Blue and Red Light

Physical principle

The refractive index depends on the wavelength. For ordinary optical glass in the visible range, the refractive index for blue light is greater than for red light. A simple converging lens therefore refracts blue light more and focuses it closer to the lens.

fblue < fred

The difference in the positions of the foci is a manifestation of longitudinal chromatic aberration. In an achromatic system the difference can be strongly reduced.

The diagram is qualitative; the difference between the foci is deliberately exaggerated.

Proposed procedure

  1. Place the illuminated object, a simple converging lens and the screen on a common optical axis. The object must be farther from the lens than its focal point so that a real image is formed on the screen.
  2. Put the red filter in front of the object. Move the screen until you obtain the sharpest image.
  3. Measure the distance of the object from the lens a and the distance of the image from the lens b. For a thin lens, measure approximately from its centre.
  4. Replace the filter with the blue one. Keep the positions of the object and the lens unchanged. Find the sharp image again and measure b.
  5. Calculate the focal length for each colour. Repeat the measurement for at least three suitable object positions.
  6. Compare the mean values and judge whether the difference is larger than the measurement uncertainty.
1 / f = 1 / a + 1 / b
ab = f(a + b)
f = ab / (a + b)

“From the object and image distances we determined the focal lengths for both colours using the lens equation. We measured … .
