1. Fluid Mechanics – Flow

1.1 Steady and unsteady flow

In steady flow the velocity, pressure and density of the liquid at any point do not change with time – all particles pass a given point with the same velocity (water in a pipe with the tap open). In unsteady flow these quantities change with time at a given point (opening a tap, liquid draining from a vessel, blood flow in arteries).

Flow is represented by streamlines – lines whose tangent at every point has the direction of the particle’s velocity. In steady flow the streamlines do not change and do not cross. A set of streamlines bounds a stream tube.

1.2 Continuity equation and Bernoulli’s equation

The continuity equation expresses the conservation of mass for an incompressible liquid. The same volume passes through every cross-section of the pipe in the same time:

QV = S₁ v₁ = S₂ v₂ = const.

In a narrower place the liquid flows faster, in a wider place more slowly.

Bernoulli’s equation expresses the conservation of mechanical energy for an ideal liquid. The sum of the pressure, kinetic and gravitational potential energy per unit volume is constant:

p + ½ ρ v² + ρ g h = const.    (horizontal pipe: p₁ + ½ρv₁² = p₂ + ½ρv₂²)

Where the liquid flows faster, its pressure is lower – the hydrodynamic paradox. Applications: Venturi tube for measuring flow speed, water-jet pump, sprayer, carburettor, lift of a wing.

1.3 Ideal and real liquid

An ideal liquid is incompressible and has no internal friction. All its layers in a pipe flow at the same speed, and the pressure does not change along a horizontal pipe of constant cross-section.

A real liquid has viscosity (internal friction). The layer at the wall does not move and the speed increases towards the axis of the pipe – the velocity profile is parabolic. Energy is used to overcome friction, so the pressure falls along a pipe of constant cross-section. At low speeds the flow is laminar (the layers do not mix), at high speeds it is turbulent (eddies form). A body in a flowing real fluid experiences a drag force.

1.4 Solution of the task

a) From the continuity equation:

S₁ v₁ = S₂ v₂   →   S₂ / S₁ = v₁ / v₂ = 8 / 4 = 2

The cross-section of the wider part is twice as large.

b) From Bernoulli’s equation for a horizontal pipe:

p₂ = p₁ + ½ ρ (v₁² − v₂²) = 1.08 · 10⁵ Pa + ½ · 1 000 · (8² − 4²) Pa
p₂ = 1.08 · 10⁵ Pa + 24 000 Pa = 1.32 · 10⁵ Pa

In the wider part the water flows more slowly; part of its kinetic energy is converted into pressure energy – the pressure rises.

S₂ = 2S₁; p₂ = 1.32 · 10⁵ Pa.

2. Change of State – Heating of a Bullet

2.1 Internal energy and heat

Internal energy of a body is the sum of the kinetic energies of the random motion of its particles and the potential energies of their mutual interaction. It changes by doing work (friction, impact, compression) or by heat transfer. Energy transferred by heat transfer is heat Q. The heat needed to change the temperature of a body:

Q = m c Δt

Specific heat capacity c is the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K [J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹].

2.2 Changes of state

A substance can pass between the solid, liquid and gaseous states: melting and freezing, evaporation (boiling) and condensation, sublimation and deposition. A crystalline substance melts at its melting point, which does not change during melting. The energy supplied breaks down the crystal lattice – it increases the potential energy of the particles. The heat needed to melt a body at its melting point:

Lt = m lt

The specific latent heat of fusion lt is the heat needed to melt 1 kg of a substance at its melting point [J·kg⁻¹]. The same heat is released on freezing.

2.3 Solution of the task

a) On impact the bullet stops abruptly and deforms. Its kinetic energy is converted – by the work of the deforming forces – into the internal energy of the bullet (and partly of the plate). The lead particles start to move faster and the temperature of the bullet rises. This is not heat transfer but a change of internal energy by doing work. The mass of the bullet cancels in the calculation.

b) If all the kinetic energy were used for heating:

½ m v² = m c Δt   →   Δt = v² / (2c) = 300² / (2 · 125) K = 90 000 / 250 K = 360 K

The bullet would reach 40 °C + 360 °C = 400 °C. That is more than the melting point of lead (327 °C). The bullet therefore heats up only to 327 °C and the rest of the energy is used for melting.

c) Minimum speed to heat up to the melting point (Δt = 327 °C − 40 °C = 287 K):

½ v² = c Δt   →   v = √(2 c Δt) = √(2 · 125 · 287) m/s = √71 750 m/s ≈ 268 m/s

d) At a higher speed the bullet reaches 327 °C and the excess energy is used for melting – the state changes while the temperature stays at 327 °C. Energy per 1 kg of the bullet:

kinetic energy: ½ v² = ½ · 300² J/kg = 45 000 J/kg
heating to 327 °C: c Δt = 125 · 287 J/kg = 35 875 J/kg
left for melting: 45 000 − 35 875 = 9 125 J/kg
melted fraction: 9 125 / 23 000 ≈ 0.40 = 40 %

To melt the bullet completely, a speed of v = √(2(cΔt + lt)) = √(2 · 58 875) m/s ≈ 343 m/s would be needed.

b) Δt = 360 K (without melting); c) v ≈ 268 m/s; d) the bullet heats up to 327 °C and about 40 % of the lead melts.

In reality part of the energy goes into the plate, sound and deformation, so the bullet warms up less. The calculation gives an upper limit.

3. Experiment – Sliding Friction and Rolling Resistance

3.1 Theory

Sliding friction arises when a body slides over the surface of another body. It is caused by the unevenness of the contact surfaces, which catch on each other, and by attractive forces between the particles of the surfaces. The frictional force acts in the contact surface against the direction of motion:

Fₜ = f · Fₙ

Fₙ is the size of the normal force with which the body presses on the surface (on a horizontal surface Fₙ = G). The coefficient of sliding friction f is dimensionless and depends on the materials and the finish of the contact surfaces (wood on wood about 0.3, steel on ice about 0.02, rubber on dry asphalt about 0.7). To set a body in motion, static friction must be overcome, which is greater than friction during motion.

Rolling resistance arises when a body rolls (a wheel, cylinder, ball), because both the body and the surface deform at the point of contact. The resistive force is

Fv = ξ · Fₙ / R

ξ is the arm of rolling resistance [m] and R the radius of the rolling body. Rolling resistance is much smaller than sliding friction – that is why wheels and ball bearings are used.

3.2 Apparatus

A wooden block with a hook whose faces have different areas and one face covered with rubber or felt, masses, a force meter (range 0–5 N), different surfaces (wooden board, glass, cloth), several round pencils or rollers, balance.

3.3 Arrangement and procedure

Pull the block horizontally with the force meter in uniform motion. Then the resultant force is zero and the force meter shows exactly the size of the frictional force (F = Fₜ). Read the force during motion, not at the start.

  1. Normal force: add masses to the block and measure Fₜ for different Fₙ = Gblock + Gmasses.
  2. Contact area: with the same load, place the block once on a large face and once on a small face.
  3. Kind of surface: at the same Fₙ change the surface and the face of the block (wood, rubber, glass).
  4. Speed: pull the block slowly and then faster. Separately measure the force needed to start it moving.

For comparison with rolling resistance, place the block on pencils and measure the pulling force during uniform motion.

3.4 Sample evaluation

The following numbers are model data for a wooden block with a weight of 2.0 N; they are not a real measurement.

a) Dependence on the normal force (wood on wood):

Fₜ (N)0.61.21.82.4
f = Fₜ/Fₙ0.300.300.300.30

The frictional force is directly proportional to the normal force – the graph is a straight line through the origin with gradient f ≈ 0.30.

b) Contact area (Fₙ = 4.0 N): large face Fₜ = 1.2 N, small face Fₜ = 1.2 N – the frictional force does not depend on the area of contact. With a smaller area the pressure is greater, so the actual area of contact of the unevennesses stays about the same.

c) Kind of surface (Fₙ = 4.0 N):

Contact surfacesFₜ (N)f

d) Speed: with slow and faster pulling the force meter shows about 1.2 N – the frictional force (at low speeds) hardly depends on speed. About 1.6 N was needed to start the block moving – static friction is greater.

Rolling resistance: the block on pencils (Fₙ = 4.0 N) could be pulled with a force of about 0.1 N, i.e. about twelve times smaller than with sliding friction.

3.5 Sources of error

  • It is difficult to pull perfectly uniformly – the force meter reading fluctuates; take the mean value from several measurements.
  • The force meter must be horizontal, otherwise the normal force changes.
  • The surfaces must be clean and dry; dust and moisture change the coefficient of friction.
Conclusion: The frictional force in sliding friction is directly proportional to the normal force (Fₜ = fFₙ), depends on the kind and finish of the contact surfaces, does not depend on their area and (at low speeds) hardly depends on speed. Static friction is greater than friction during motion, and rolling resistance is much smaller than sliding friction.
