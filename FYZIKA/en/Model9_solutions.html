1. Changes of State and Internal Energy

1.1 Overview of changes of state

ChangeDirectionHeat transfer
MeltingSolid → liquidAbsorbs heat
SolidificationLiquid → solidReleases heat
Evaporation, boilingLiquid → gasAbsorbs heat
CondensationGas → liquidReleases heat
SublimationSolid → gasAbsorbs heat
DepositionGas → solidReleases heat

Evaporation takes place at the surface of a liquid at any temperature at which the liquid phase exists. Mainly the particles with sufficiently high energy escape, so the liquid can cool down. The rate of evaporation is affected by temperature, surface area, air flow and the amount of vapour above the surface.

Boiling takes place throughout the volume: bubbles of vapour form. It occurs when the saturated vapour pressure reaches the pressure acting on the liquid. The boiling point therefore depends on pressure; at lower pressure it is lower.

1.2 Explanation in terms of particles

In a solid, the particles vibrate about equilibrium positions. In a liquid they remain close together but change neighbours and positions. In a gas they are generally far apart and move more freely. Internal energy includes the kinetic energy of random motion and the potential energy of the interaction between particles.

When a pure crystalline substance melts at constant pressure, the temperature remains constant during the change. The energy supplied is used to change the arrangement and interaction of the particles; in the school model it is mainly the potential part of the internal energy that increases. A constant temperature therefore does not mean a constant internal energy. During solidification energy is released. Amorphous substances soften over a range of temperatures.

Q = mL    [L] = J/kg

The specific latent heat L expresses the heat needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance under the given conditions. For melting we use Lf, for vaporisation Lv.

1.3 Phase diagram

A phase diagram shows the equilibrium phases of a substance as a function of the thermodynamic temperature T and the pressure p. The regions represent the individual phases; on the boundary curves two phases are in equilibrium.

Triple point: at a single combination of temperature and pressure, the solid, liquid and gas phases coexist. Below the triple-point pressure, the liquid phase is not stable in equilibrium; a heated solid can sublime.

Critical point: ends the liquid–vapour equilibrium curve. Above the critical temperature, a gas cannot be liquefied by increasing the pressure alone. At temperatures and pressures above the critical values we speak of a supercritical fluid.

The diagram is schematic, not to scale. For most substances the melting curve has a positive slope. For water it has a negative slope in the usual range: increasing the pressure lowers the melting point of ice.

1.4 Melting of tin – calculation

Given: m = 3 kg, t = tm = 232 °C, Lf = 61 kJ/kg = 61 000 J/kg.

The tin is already at its melting point, so no heat is needed to warm the solid. Heat is needed only for complete melting:

Q = mLf = 3 · 61 000 = 183 000 J = 183 kJ

The first law of thermodynamics in the form ΔU = Q − W, where W is the work done by the system. According to the problem, we neglect the work associated with the change in volume:

ΔU ≈ Q = +183 kJ. The internal energy of the tin increases by 183 kJ; the temperature remains 232 °C during melting.

2. Electron Shell and Acceleration of an Electron

2.1 Electron shell

The electron shell consists of electrons bound to the nucleus by the electromagnetic interaction. In an atom they have allowed discrete energy levels. An orbital describes a quantum state and the spatial probability distribution of the electron; it is not a classical orbit.

When passing to a lower energy level, an atom can emit a photon. On absorbing a photon of the corresponding energy, it can pass to a higher level:

Ephoton = hf = Ehigher − Elower

This gives rise to line emission and absorption spectra. If enough energy is supplied, an electron can leave the atom – ionisation occurs.

2.2 Speed of the electron

Given: U = 100 V, v₀ = 0, |q| = e = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C, me = 9.109 · 10⁻³¹ kg.

The electron has a negative charge and the electric force acts against the direction of the field strength. On acceleration it gains kinetic energy equal to the work done by the electric force. U here denotes the magnitude of the accelerating voltage:

eU = ½mev²   →   v = √(2eU/me)
v = √[(2 · 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ · 100)/(9.109 · 10⁻³¹)]
= √(3.517 · 10¹³) m/s ≈ 5.93 · 10⁶ m/s
The speed of the electron is approximately 5.93 · 10⁶ m/s. The kinetic energy is 100 eV = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁷ J.

The speed is about 2 % of the speed of light, so a non-relativistic calculation is sufficient.

2.3 de Broglie wavelength

A particle with momentum p has an associated de Broglie wavelength. At a speed small compared with the speed of light, p = mev:

λ = h/p = h/(mev) = h/√(2meeU)
p = 9.109 · 10⁻³¹ · 5.93 · 10⁶ ≈ 5.40 · 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s
λ = (6.626 · 10⁻³⁴)/(5.40 · 10⁻²⁴)
≈ 1.23 · 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.123 nm
The de Broglie wavelength is approximately 0.123 nm.

This wavelength characterises the wave behaviour of the electron, for example diffraction; it is neither the size of the electron nor the wavelength of an emitted photon.

3. Experiment – Progressive Transverse Wave

Equipment and arrangement

A longer soft rope or cord and a coloured marker attached at a chosen point. The rope is slightly stretched between two people or between a hand and a support.

Procedure and observation

  1. With one short up-and-down movement of the hand we create a wave pulse. We watch it travel gradually along the rope.
  2. We watch the coloured marker: it moves transversely, not along with the pulse towards the end of the rope.
  3. We move the hand up and down regularly. A train of waves forms and travels along the rope. We observe them before the reflection returns; the second person can damp the end.
  4. We increase the frequency of oscillation at approximately the same tension. At approximately the same wave speed, the wavelength decreases.

Explanation

Progressive means that the disturbance is transferred from place to place. Transverse means that the particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation. These are two different characteristics: a progressive wave can be transverse or longitudinal.

Each part of the rope acts on the neighbouring parts and transfers the disturbance and the energy. The rope as a whole does not travel with the propagating wave. The velocity of oscillation of a point on the rope is not the speed of the wave.

v = λf = λ/T

For a steady harmonic wave, f is the frequency of the source, T the period and λ the distance between the nearest points with the same phase, for example two adjacent crests. The wave speed on the rope depends on its tension and its mass per unit length.

The reflection at the end of the rope can combine with the incident wave to form a standing wave. That is why, when demonstrating a progressive wave, we first watch the first pulse or damp the reflections.

Conclusion: The disturbance and the energy travel along the rope; its particles oscillate transversely about their equilibrium positions.
