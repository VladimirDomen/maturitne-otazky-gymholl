1. Electric Current

1.1 Electric current as a phenomenon

Electric current is the ordered (directed) motion of free charged particles. It requires:

  • free charge carriers – free electrons in metals, ions in electrolytes, ions and electrons in gases, electrons and holes in semiconductors,
  • an electric field in the material, i.e. a voltage between the ends of the conductor – supplied by a source,
  • a closed electric circuit (for a steady current).

The quantity electric current I gives the charge that passes through a cross-section of the conductor per unit time:

I = Q/t    [I] = A (ampere, SI base unit)

The conventional direction of current is the direction of motion of positive charges, i.e. from the positive terminal of the source through the external circuit to the negative terminal. Electrons in metals move in the opposite direction. Their drift velocity is very small (fractions of a millimetre per second). However, the electric field spreads through the circuit almost at the speed of light, so the current flows in the whole circuit practically instantly.

Current shows itself through its effects: heating (cooker, bulb, fuse), magnetic (electromagnet, motor), chemical (electrolysis, charging an accumulator) and light (discharge lamp, LED). By its time dependence we distinguish direct and alternating current.

1.2 Voltage sources

A source converts another form of energy into electrical energy. By non-electric (external) forces it separates positive and negative charges and maintains a voltage between its terminals:

  • galvanic cells and accumulators – chemical energy (battery, car battery, Li-ion cell),
  • generators (alternators, dynamos) – mechanical energy via electromagnetic induction (power stations),
  • photovoltaic cells – light energy,
  • thermocouples – internal energy (temperature difference between two junctions of different metals),
  • piezoelectric sources – mechanical deformation of a crystal (lighter).

1.3 Electromotive force and terminal voltage

The electromotive force Ue is the ratio of the work Wz done by the non-electric forces of the source in moving a charge Q inside the source to this charge:

Ue = Wz/Q

It is a characteristic of the source and does not depend on the connected circuit.

The terminal voltage U is the voltage between the terminals of the source when it supplies a current. The current also passes through the source, which has an internal resistance Ri, and a voltage drop RiI appears across it:

U = Ue − Ri I     I = Ue/(R + Ri)

The second relation is Ohm’s law for a complete circuit.

  • On open circuit (I = 0) the terminal voltage equals the emf: U = Ue. It is measured approximately with a voltmeter of high resistance.
  • The greater the current drawn, the smaller the terminal voltage.
  • Short circuit (R = 0): U = 0 and the current is maximum, Ik = Ue/Ri. This is dangerous with accumulators.

1.4 Solution of the task

Given: t = 2 min = 120 s, Q = 30 C, e = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C.

a) Electric current:

I = Q/t = 30 C / 120 s = 0.25 A

b) Charge is a whole multiple of the elementary charge, Q = Ne · e:

Ne = Q/e = 30 C / 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 1.87 · 10²⁰

c) Terminal voltage and the resistance of the external part of the circuit:

U = Ue − Ri I = 4.5 V − 1 Ω · 0.25 A = 4.25 V
R = U/I = 4.25 V / 0.25 A = 17 Ω

Check with Ohm’s law for a complete circuit: I = Ue/(R + Ri) = 4.5 V / 18 Ω = 0.25 A.

I = 0.25 A; Ne ≈ 1.9 · 10²⁰ electrons; U = 4.25 V, R = 17 Ω.

2. Kinematics – v–t Graph

2.1 Theory

Uniform motion in a straight line: the velocity is constant, the acceleration is zero:

v = const.    s = v t    a = 0

Uniformly accelerated (decelerated) motion in a straight line: the acceleration is constant, the velocity changes linearly with time:

a = Δv/Δt = const.    v = v₀ + a t    s = v₀ t + ½ a t²

In decelerated motion the acceleration is directed opposite to the velocity (a < 0).

From the v(t) graph: the acceleration is the gradient of the graph a = Δv/Δt. The distance equals the area under the graph (if the body keeps moving in one direction). For motion with constant acceleration it is the area of a trapezium, s = ½ (v₁ + v₂) Δt.

2.2 Type of motion and accelerations

SectionTimeVelocityMotionAcceleration
I0 – 3 s4 m/s constantuniforma₁ = 0
II3 – 7 s4 → 8 m/suniformly accelerateda₂ = (8 − 4)/(7 − 3) = 1 m/s²
III7 – 15 s8 → 0 m/suniformly decelerateda₃ = (0 − 8)/(15 − 7) = −1 m/s²

At 15 s the body stops.

2.3 Distances

The distances are the areas under the graph:

s₁ = v t₁ = 4 m/s · 3 s = 12 m (rectangle)
s₂ = ½ (4 + 8) m/s · 4 s = 24 m (trapezium)
s₃ = ½ · 8 m/s · 8 s = 32 m (triangle)

The equations for accelerated motion give the same result, e.g. s₂ = v₀t + ½at² = 4 · 4 + ½ · 1 · 4² = 24 m and s₃ = 8 · 8 − ½ · 1 · 8² = 32 m.

s = s₁ + s₂ + s₃ = 12 m + 24 m + 32 m = 68 m

Average speed over the whole distance: vp = s/t = 68 m / 15 s ≈ 4.5 m/s.

2.4 Graph s(t)

  • 0 – 3 s: s = 4t, a straight line. At 3 s, s = 12 m.
  • 3 – 7 s: s = 12 + 4(t − 3) + ½(t − 3)², a parabola; the graph becomes steeper. At 7 s, s = 36 m.
  • 7 – 15 s: s = 36 + 8(t − 7) − ½(t − 7)², a parabola; the graph becomes less steep. At 15 s, s = 68 m and the tangent is horizontal (v = 0).

The graph is continuous with no kinks: the gradient of s(t) is the velocity, which does not change abruptly.

2.5 Graph a(t)

The acceleration is constant in each section, so the graph a(t) consists of horizontal segments: 0, then 1 m/s², then −1 m/s². After the body stops the acceleration is zero again. At 3 s, 7 s and 15 s the acceleration changes abruptly (an idealisation).

a₁ = 0, a₂ = 1 m/s², a₃ = −1 m/s²; s₁ = 12 m, s₂ = 24 m, s₃ = 32 m, in total s = 68 m.

3. Experiment – Wavelength of Light

3.1 Principle – diffraction of light by a grating

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Visible light has a wavelength of roughly 380–760 nm, which is too small to measure directly. We use diffraction and interference of light on a diffraction grating.

A diffraction grating is a set of a large number of parallel slits at equal spacing. The distance between neighbouring slits is the grating spacing d. If the grating has N lines per 1 mm, then d = 1 mm / N.

Light is diffracted behind each slit. The waves from the individual slits combine, and bright maxima appear in directions in which the path difference of neighbouring rays is a whole multiple of the wavelength:

d · sin αk = k · λ     k = 0, 1, 2, …

k is the order of the maximum and αk the angle at which the maximum is seen. If the screen is at a distance L and the k-th order maximum is at a distance xk from the central maximum, then:

tan αk = xk/L    →    λ = d · sin αk / k

For small angles sin α ≈ tan α, so λ ≈ d xk/(k L).

3.2 Apparatus

Laser pointer (e.g. red), diffraction grating with a known number of lines (e.g. 300 lines per mm), stands, screen (a wall with paper), tape measure or long ruler, pencil. A CD (track spacing 1.6 μm) used in reflection can replace the grating.

3.3 Arrangement

The laser beam falls perpendicularly on the grating and the screen is parallel to the grating. On the screen we see a row of bright spots: the central maximum (k = 0) and, symmetrically on both sides, maxima of the 1st, 2nd, … order.

3.4 Procedure

  1. Fix the laser and the grating so that the beam falls on the grating perpendicularly. Place the screen at a distance L (e.g. 1 m) and measure this distance.
  2. Mark the positions of the maxima on the paper. Measure the distance 2xk between the symmetrical maxima of the same order – this is more accurate than finding the centre.
  3. Repeat the measurement for the 2nd-order maxima and for another distance L.
  4. For each measurement calculate tan αk = xk/L, the angle αk, sin αk and λ = d sin αk/k. Average the results.

Average: λ ≈ 649 nm. The manufacturer states 650 nm for the red laser – the result agrees well.

For 2nd-order maxima the angle is larger and sin α ≈ tan α no longer holds (0.390 versus 0.423). The calculation must therefore go through the angle, not just λ ≈ dx/(kL).

3.6 Sources of error and safety

Never look into the laser beam and never point it at people. Use a class 2 laser (up to 1 mW) and watch out for reflections from shiny surfaces. Errors come from locating the maxima (they are spots of a certain width), measuring L and non-perpendicular incidence of the beam. Accuracy improves with a larger distance L and by measuring higher orders.

If white light is used instead of a laser, each maximum (except the central one) splits into a spectrum. Violet is closest to the centre and red farthest away, because a longer wavelength corresponds to a larger angle. Grating spectroscopes work on this principle.

Conclusion: The wavelength is found from d sin αk = kλ, where tan αk = xk/L. For a red laser λ ≈ 650 nm.
