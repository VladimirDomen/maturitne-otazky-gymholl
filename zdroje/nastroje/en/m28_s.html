1. Optical Imaging

1.1 Principles of imaging

An optical system (a mirror, a lens) changes the direction of the rays coming from the points of an object. If the rays after reflection or refraction really meet at one point, a real image is formed – it can be caught on a screen. If only their extensions meet, a virtual image is formed – we can see it, but it does not appear on a screen.

We construct the image using principal rays. For spherical mirrors and thin lenses we consider only rays close to the optical axis (paraxial rays); otherwise the image is blurred.

A plane mirror forms a virtual, upright image of the same size, symmetric to the object with respect to the plane of the mirror.

1.2 Mirror equation

1/a + 1/a′ = 1/f     f = r/2

a is the object distance (object – vertex of the mirror), a′ the image distance, f the focal length and r the radius of curvature. A concave mirror has its focus in front of the mirror, a convex mirror behind it.

1.3 Imaging with lenses

A lens forms images by refraction of light at two spherical surfaces. A converging lens is thicker in the middle; parallel rays meet after refraction at the image focus F′. A diverging lens is thinner in the middle; parallel rays leave it diverging, as if coming from a focus in front of the lens. The same imaging equation 1/a + 1/a′ = 1/f applies to a thin lens.

Principal rays of a converging lens:

  • a ray parallel to the optical axis is refracted through the image focus F′,
  • a ray through the optical centre O passes without changing direction,
  • a ray through the object focus F is refracted parallel to the axis.

1.4 Sign convention and lateral magnification

  • a > 0 for a real object (in front of the mirror or lens),
  • a′ > 0 for a real image, a′ < 0 for a virtual image,
  • f > 0 for a concave mirror and a converging lens, f < 0 for a convex mirror and a diverging lens,
  • a size above the optical axis is positive, below it negative.

Lateral magnification is the ratio of the image size y′ to the object size y:

Z = y′/y = −a′/a

Z > 0 means an upright image, Z < 0 an inverted one. |Z| > 1 means a magnified image, |Z| < 1 a diminished one.

2. Electric Field

2.1 Field strength of a point charge

The electric field strength of a point charge Q at a distance r has magnitude:

E = k|Q|/r²     k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 9 · 10⁹ N·m²/C²

The field vector points away from a positive charge and towards a negative one. The resultant field of several charges is the vector sum of the fields of the individual charges (principle of superposition).

2.2 Solution of the task

{{SVG1}}

a) Point M is at r = d/2 = 0.105 m from both charges:

E₁ = kQ₁/r² = 9 · 10⁹ · 2 · 10⁻⁸ / 0.105² N/C ≈ 1.63 · 10⁴ N/C
E₂ = kQ₂/r² = 9 · 10⁹ · 8 · 10⁻⁸ / 0.105² N/C ≈ 6.53 · 10⁴ N/C

Both charges are positive, so at M the field E₁ points away from Q₁ (towards Q₂) and E₂ away from Q₂ (towards Q₁) – they are opposite:

E = E₂ − E₁ ≈ 4.9 · 10⁴ N/C

The resultant field points from the larger charge Q₂ towards the smaller charge Q₁.

b) The fields can cancel only where they are opposite – between the charges. Point N is at a distance x from Q₁:

kQ₁/x² = kQ₂/(d − x)²   →   (d − x)/x = √(Q₂/Q₁) = 2   →   x = d/3 = 7 cm

The zero point is closer to the smaller charge – 7 cm from Q₁ and 14 cm from Q₂.

c) If, for example, Q₂ were negative, the fields at M would have the same direction (from Q₁ towards Q₂) and would add up:

E = E₁ + E₂ ≈ 8.2 · 10⁴ N/C

There would be no zero point between the charges. The fields would cancel outside the segment, on the side of the smaller charge Q₁, at a distance x from it:

(d + x)/x = 2   →   x = d = 21 cm

3. Experiment – Specific Heat Capacity of a Solid

3.1 Principle

We put a heated body into cold water in a calorimeter. The body cools down and the water and the calorimeter warm up until the equilibrium temperature t is reached. Neglecting heat exchange with the surroundings, the calorimetric equation applies – the heat given by the body equals the heat received by the water and the calorimeter:

mtct(tt − t) = (mvcv + Ck)(t − tv)

From this the required specific heat capacity is:

ct = (mvcv + Ck)(t − tv) / [mt(tt − t)]    [c] = J/(kg·K)

Index t refers to the body, v to the water and k to the calorimeter.

3.2 Equipment

A mixing calorimeter with a stirrer, a thermometer (preferably digital), scales, a metal body on a thread, a beaker of boiling water or a water bath, cold water.

{{SVG2}}

3.3 Procedure

  1. Weigh the body (mt) and the water in the calorimeter (mv). The heat capacity of the calorimeter Ck is known or determined beforehand.
  2. Measure the initial temperature of the water tv.
  3. Heat the body in boiling water for several minutes; its temperature tt equals the temperature of the bath.
  4. Quickly transfer the body into the calorimeter, cover it and stir.
  5. Record the highest steady temperature t and calculate ct.

3.5 Sources of error

The body cools during the transfer and carries some hot water with it. The calorimeter exchanges heat with the surroundings, the thermometer responds with a delay and the water may not be well mixed. The error is reduced by a quick transfer, covering the calorimeter, thorough stirring and preparing the cold water slightly below room temperature – heat losses and gains then partly balance out.
