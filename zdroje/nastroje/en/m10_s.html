1. Structure and Properties of Gases

Model of an ideal gas

A gas consists of a large number of particles in constant random motion. In the ideal gas model we neglect the volume of the particles themselves and their mutual interaction except during collisions. Collisions between particles and with the walls of the container are considered perfectly elastic. Real gases approach the model at sufficiently low pressure and high temperature, far from liquefaction.

Root-mean-square speed

vrms = √[(v₁² + v₂² + … + vN²)/N]
vrms = √(3kT/m₀) = √(3RmT/M)

k is the Boltzmann constant, T the thermodynamic temperature, m₀ the mass of one molecule, Rm the molar gas constant and M the molar mass. The temperature is substituted in kelvins. For the same gas vrms ∝ √T; at the same temperature lighter molecules have a greater root-mean-square speed.

Mean translational kinetic energy of a molecule: E̅k = ½m₀vrms² = ³⁄₂kT

Pressure of an ideal gas

Pressure results from the impacts of molecules on the walls of the container and the transfer of momentum to the wall.

p = ⅓(N/V)m₀vrms² = ⅓ρvrms²

N is the number of molecules, V the volume and ρ the density of the gas. Substituting m₀vrms² = 3kT gives pV = NkT.

Equation of state

pV = NkT = nRmT = (m/M)RmT
For a fixed amount of gas: p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂

n is the amount of substance, m the mass of the gas and Rm ≈ 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹. In the equation we use the absolute pressure and the temperature in kelvins: T = t + 273.15.

Energy point of view

We use the convention: Q > 0 when the gas absorbs heat; W > 0 when the gas does work while expanding.

Q = ΔU + W; at constant pressure W = p(V₂ − V₁)

For a given amount and composition, the internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature. For a monatomic ideal gas U = ³⁄₂nRmT.

  • Isothermal process: ΔU = 0, so Q = W. On expansion the gas absorbs heat and does work.
  • Isochoric process: W = 0, so Q = ΔU. The heat supplied increases the internal energy, the temperature and the pressure.
  • Isobaric process: Q = ΔU + pΔV. On heating, part of the heat supplied increases the internal energy and part is used for the work done by the gas.
  • Adiabatic process: Q = 0, so ΔU = −W. On expansion the gas cools, on compression it warms up.

2. Air Pressure in a Tyre

Given: p₁ = 250 kPa, t₁ = 17 °C, t₂ = 77 °C, V = const., the amount of air does not change.

T₁ = 17 + 273.15 = 290.15 K
T₂ = 77 + 273.15 = 350.15 K

From the equation of state at constant volume:

p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂
p₂ = p₁ · T₂/T₁ = 250 · 350.15/290.15 kPa
p₂ ≈ 301.70 kPa
The final absolute pressure is approximately 302 kPa. It has increased by approximately 51.7 kPa, i.e. by 20.7 %.

If 250 kPa meant gauge pressure: we would first add the atmospheric pressure. With patm = 101.3 kPa, p₁ = 351.3 kPa and p₂ ≈ 423.95 kPa. The final gauge pressure would be approximately 322.65 kPa ≈ 323 kPa. The problem above explicitly states the absolute pressure.

3. Concave Spherical Mirror

Given: R = 4 cm, a = 1 cm, y = 0.5 cm. Distances are measured from the vertex V of the mirror. The centre of curvature C is at a distance R from the vertex.

Focal length

f = R/2 = 4/2 cm = 2 cm

The object lies between the vertex of the mirror and the focus, so we expect a virtual, upright and magnified image.

Geometric construction

  1. Draw the optical axis, the vertex V, the focus F 2 cm in front of the mirror and the centre of curvature C 4 cm in front of the mirror.
  2. Draw the object 1 cm in front of the mirror with a height of 0.5 cm.
  3. From the top of the object draw a ray parallel to the axis. After reflection it passes through the focus F.
  4. Draw a second ray to the vertex V of the mirror. Reflect it at the same angle with respect to the optical axis, which is the normal at the vertex.
  5. The reflected rays diverge in front of the mirror. Their extensions behind the mirror intersect at the top of the virtual image.

The drawing uses the paraxial approximation of the spherical mirror, as does the mirror equation below.

Position of the image

With the sign convention used, f > 0 for a concave mirror, a > 0 for an object in front of the mirror and a′ < 0 for a virtual image behind the mirror.

1/f = 1/a + 1/a′
1/a′ = 1/f − 1/a = 1/2 − 1 = −1/2 cm⁻¹
a′ = −2 cm

Magnification and height of the image

Z = y′/y = −a′/a = −(−2)/1 = 2
y′ = Z · y = 2 · 0.5 cm = 1 cm
The image is 2 cm behind the mirror, has a height of 1 cm and is virtual, upright and magnified twice. It cannot be caught on a screen.
