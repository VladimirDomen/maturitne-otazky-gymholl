1. Molecular Physics and Thermodynamics

1.1 Foundations of molecular physics

Substances consist of particles (atoms, molecules and ions). The particles move constantly and randomly and interact with each other. At a higher temperature, the mean kinetic energy of their random motion generally increases. Molecular physics explains the macroscopic properties of substances by the microscopic behaviour of particles.

1.2 Temperature scales

The Celsius temperature t is given in °C, the thermodynamic temperature T in kelvins (K). It holds that:

T = (t / °C + 273.15) K

For example, 20 °C corresponds to 293.15 K. A temperature difference of 1 °C equals a difference of 1 K. Absolute zero is 0 K = −273.15 °C.

1.3 Internal energy

The internal energy U is the sum of the microscopic kinetic energies of the particles and the potential energies of their mutual interaction. It changes by doing work or by heat transfer.

1.4 First law of thermodynamics

ΔU = Q + Won

Q is the heat received by the system, Won the work done on the system. Using the work Wby done by the system, the same balance reads ΔU = Q − Wby. The law expresses the conservation of energy in thermodynamic processes.

1.5 Calorimetric equation

In a thermally insulated system, the sum of the heats received and given off is zero. When two substances are mixed without a change of state and neglecting the heat capacity of the vessel:

m₁c₁(t − t₁) + m₂c₂(t − t₂) = 0

The relation follows from Q = mcΔt. In general, the heat received by the calorimeter and any latent heats are also included.

1.6 Amount of substance

Number of particles N = 4.8 · 10²⁴. Avogadro constant NA = 6.02214076 · 10²³ mol⁻¹.

n = N / NA = (4.8 · 10²⁴) / (6.022 · 10²³) ≈ 7.97 mol
Result: n ≈ 8.0 mol of hydrogen atoms. This is the amount of atoms, not of H₂ molecules.

2. Electromagnetic Waves – Solution

Given: A = 20 V, f = 10 MHz = 10⁷ Hz, v = 3 · 10⁸ m/s, x = 3 m, t = 10⁻³ s.

For a progressive wave travelling in the positive direction of the x-axis with zero initial phase:

u(x,t) = A sin(ωt − kx) = A sin[2πf(t − x/v)]

Angular frequency and wavelength:

ω = 2πf = 2π · 10⁷ rad/s
λ = v/f = 30 m  →  k = 2π/30 rad/m

Equation of the progressive wave (with x in metres and t in seconds):

u(x,t) = 20 sin(2π · 10⁷t − 2πx/30) V

Substituting the time and distance:

u(3; 10⁻³) = 20 sin[2π(10⁴ − 0.1)]
= 20 sin(−π/5) ≈ −11.76 V
The instantaneous value of the voltage is approximately −11.8 V. The sign expresses the polarity relative to the chosen reference.

The sine form used assumes zero initial phase. With a different initial phase, the instantaneous value would be different.

3. Experiment – Verifying the Period of a Simple Pendulum

Theory

A simple pendulum is modelled as a point mass on a light, inextensible string of length l. For small displacements (up to about 10°):

T = 2π√(l/g)    and hence    T² = (4π²/g)l

The period depends on the length of the string and the gravitational acceleration; in this approximation it does not depend on the mass of the bob.

{{SVG0}}

Equipment

A stand, strong thread, a small spherical bob, a metre rule or tape measure, a stopwatch and possibly a protractor.

Procedure

  1. Set up the pendulum and measure the length from the point of suspension to the centre of mass of the bob.
  2. Displace the bob by a small angle (e.g. 5–10°) and release it without pushing.
  3. Measure the time t10 for 10 complete oscillations and calculate T = t10/10.
  4. Repeat the measurement and carry it out for several lengths l.
  5. Calculate T² and compare with the theory; plot the graph of T² against l.

Evaluation and graph

From the relation T² = (4π²/g)l we expect a straight line passing approximately through the origin. Its gradient a = ΔT²/Δl has the unit s²/m and allows us to determine the gravitational acceleration:

g = 4π²/a
{{SVG1}}

For example, for a length l = 1.00 m and a period T = 2.01 s:

g = 4π²l/T² ≈ 9.77 m/s²

The result is close to the expected value of 9.81 m/s². A more accurate evaluation is obtained from the regression line of all points, not from a single measurement.

Sources of error

Reaction time when using the stopwatch, error in measuring the length, too large an initial displacement, air resistance, friction at the pivot or pushing the bob on release. Timing several oscillations and repeating the experiments reduces the relative effect of the timing error.

Conclusion: The experiment confirms that, for small displacements, T² is directly proportional to the length of the pendulum l.
