1. Mechanics of a Rigid Body

1.1 Moment of a force

A rigid body is an idealised model in which the distances between its points do not change. A force can cause both translational and rotational motion. The turning effect of a force about a chosen axis is expressed by the moment of the force.

M = F · r⊥ = F · r · sin α   [N·m]

Here r⊥ denotes the moment arm: the shortest distance from the axis of rotation to the line of action of the force; α is the angle between the position vector and the force. The sign of the moment is determined by the agreed positive sense of rotation.

1.2 Principle of moments and equilibrium

The moment of the resultant force about a point or axis is the sum of the moments of the individual forces about the same point or axis (Varignon’s theorem).

Mres = Σ Mi

If the angular velocity of the body does not change, then in equilibrium ΣM = 0. For complete static equilibrium of a rigid body, ΣF⃗ = 0 is also required. For example, for a lever in equilibrium: F₁r₁ = F₂r₂ if the moments act in opposite senses.

1.3 Composition of forces acting at different points

Forces are vectors, so their magnitudes and directions are combined by vector addition. For a rigid body, however, the lines of action also matter, because they determine the moments of the forces.

  • Forces with a common point of action: we use the parallelogram of forces or successive addition of vectors.
  • Non-parallel forces in a plane: we extend their lines of action to their intersection, move the forces along their lines to the intersection and construct the resultant.
  • Parallel forces: the resultant force is the vector sum, and its point of action is chosen so that it has the same moment as the original forces.
  • Opposite parallel forces of equal magnitude with different lines of action: they form a couple, which cannot be replaced by a single resultant force.
{{SVG0}}

When composing forces, the magnitude, direction and point of action of the resultant must be determined.

For two parallel forces in the same direction a distance d apart:

F = F₁ + F₂,   F₁·x = F₂·(d − x)

x is the distance of the resultant from F₁. If F₁ = 20 N, F₂ = 30 N and d = 1 m, the resultant has a magnitude of 50 N and lies at a distance x = 30/50 = 0.6 m from F₁.

1.4 How to determine the resultant graphically

  1. Draw the lines of action of the individual forces and their directions.
  2. If the lines intersect, find the intersection and use the parallelogram of forces or a vector triangle.
  3. If they are parallel, add their vectors and determine the position of the resultant from the equality of moments (or by a graphical procedure with auxiliary forces).
  4. Check the magnitude, the direction and the resultant moment. A zero resultant force does not necessarily mean a zero moment.

1.5 Centre of gravity of a rigid body

The centre of gravity is the point at which, when describing the effect of an approximately uniform gravitational field, we can consider the resultant weight of the body to act. For a uniformly distributed mass with geometric symmetry, it lies at the centre of symmetry. It can also lie outside the material of the body, for example in a ring.

Practical determination of the centre of gravity of a flat plate: hang the plate from one point and mark the vertical line through the point of suspension; repeat for a second point. The intersection of the vertical lines is the centre of gravity (in a uniform gravitational field).

1.6 Couple

A couple consists of two parallel forces of equal magnitude and opposite direction acting along different lines. The resultant force is zero, but their resultant moment is not zero.

{{SVG1}}

A couple has zero resultant force but a non-zero resultant moment M = Fd.

F⃗₁ + F⃗₂ = 0,   M = F · d

d is the perpendicular distance between the lines of action of the forces. Example: turning a steering wheel with both hands.

2. Alternating Current – Worked Problem

At the phase φ = π/6, the instantaneous value of a harmonic alternating voltage is u = 155 V. Determine the amplitude and the rms value.

For a harmonic voltage with zero mean value (and the phase measured from a zero crossing):

u = Um · sin φ

We know that sin(π/6) = 1/2, therefore

155 = Um · ½   ⇒   Um = 310 V

The rms value of a sinusoidal voltage:

Urms = Um / √2 = 310 / √2 ≈ 219.2 V
Result: amplitude 310 V and rms value 219.2 V.

The rms value is the value of a direct voltage that would produce the same average heating power in the same resistor as the given alternating voltage.

3. Experiment – Measuring the Refractive Index of Light

The task refers to the procedure from laboratory practicals. What follows is one possible solution; another correct laboratory procedure can also be used in the examination.

3.1 Physical principle

At a plane boundary between two transparent media, light changes direction according to Snell’s law:

n₁ sin α = n₂ sin β

If light passes from air (n₁ ≈ 1) into glass or acrylic, the refractive index of the sample is approximately:

n₂ ≈ sin α / sin β

The angles are measured from the normal to the boundary. The refractive index is dimensionless.

{{SVG2}}

The angles α and β are always measured from the normal to the boundary, not from its surface. The diagram is illustrative.

3.2 Equipment

A low-power school laser or a ray box, a semicircular transparent block (e.g. acrylic), paper with an angle scale, a ruler, a pencil and a calculator. Never point the laser into anyone’s eyes or at reflective surfaces.

3.3 Proposed procedure

  1. On the paper, draw the flat face of the block and the normal at the point of incidence.
  2. Aim a narrow beam at the flat face of the semicircular block so that after refraction it travels from the centre of curvature approximately radially through the curved face. As a result, no further significant refraction occurs at the curved face.
  3. For several angles of incidence α (e.g. 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°, 60°), draw the incident and the refracted ray.
  4. Measure α and β from the normal and, in a table, calculate sin α, sin β and their ratio.
  5. Determine the mean refractive index or plot a graph of sin α against sin β.

3.5 Graphical evaluation

Plot sin β on the horizontal axis and sin α on the vertical axis. According to Snell’s law, for light passing from air, sin α ≈ n₂ sin β. The points therefore lie approximately on a straight line through the origin, and the gradient of the line gives the refractive index n₂.

3.6 Conclusion and possible errors

Sample conclusion: By experimental measurement we obtained a refractive index of approximately n = 1.49 for the sample, a value typical of some transparent plastics.

The most common sources of uncertainty: a thick beam spot, inaccurate drawing of the normal, reading the angles, displacement of the block and aiming the beam off the centre of the curved face. A more accurate result is obtained by measuring more angles and processing the data graphically.
