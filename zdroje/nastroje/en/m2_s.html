1. Gravitational Field

1. Gravitational field

A gravitational field is the region in which a body with mass exerts a gravitational force on other bodies. The gravitational force is attractive. The field is described, for example, by the field strength K (gravitational force per unit mass):

K = Fg / m [N/kg]

The field strength is a vector; its direction is the direction of the gravitational force on a test body.

2. Newton’s law of gravitation

Two bodies with mass attract each other with a force directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their distance (for point masses or spherically symmetric bodies).

Fg = G · m1m2 / r²

G is the gravitational constant (approximately 6.67 × 10−11 N·m²/kg²), m₁, m₂ are the masses and r is the distance between their centres for spherically symmetric bodies.

3. Uniform and radial field

Uniform field: the field strength vector has approximately the same magnitude and direction throughout the region considered; the field lines are parallel (a good approximation near the Earth’s surface over a small area).

Radial field: the field lines point towards the centre of the source body, and outside a spherically symmetric body the magnitude of the field strength decreases with the square of the distance.

4. The Earth’s gravitational field

K(r) = GME / r²

At the Earth’s surface r = R, so K₀ = GME/R². Above the surface r = R + h, therefore:

K(h) = K₀ · R²/(R+h)²

In the elementary model outside the Earth, the field strength decreases with increasing distance from the centre. At every point of the field, the gravitational acceleration is numerically equal to the field strength (the units m/s² and N/kg are equivalent).

5. Kepler’s laws

  1. First: the planets orbit the Sun in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
  2. Second: the line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals.
  3. Third: for bodies orbiting the same central body, T²/a³ = const., where a is the semi-major axis of the orbit.

6. Solution of the calculation

Given: K₀ = 10 N/kg, Earth’s radius R.

Variant A: height h = 3R above the surface

First express the distance from the centre: r = R + h = R + 3R = 4R.

K = K₀ · (R/4R)² = 10/16 = 0.625 N/kg

Variant B: distance r = 3R from the centre

Here we do not add the radius, because the distance is already measured from the centre.

K = K₀ · (R/3R)² = 10/9 ≈ 1.11 N/kg
Common mistake: The height h above the surface and the distance r from the centre are not the same. Before substituting, always determine r = R + h if a height is given.

2. Mechanical Waves

1. Origin and types of waves

A mechanical wave is the propagation of a disturbance through a material medium. The particles of the medium oscillate about their equilibrium positions and pass energy on to neighbouring particles; in an ideal wave the material as a whole does not travel with the wave.

Transverse wave: the displacement of the particles is perpendicular to the direction of propagation (e.g. a wave on a stretched rope). Longitudinal wave: the displacement of the particles is parallel to the direction of propagation (e.g. sound in air).

2. Equation of a progressive harmonic wave

For a wave travelling in the positive direction of the x-axis we can write:

y(x,t) = A sin[2π(t/T − x/λ) + φ₀]

Equivalent form: y(x,t) = A sin(ωt − kx + φ₀), where ω = 2π/T = 2πf and k = 2π/λ. Basic quantities: y – instantaneous displacement (m), A – amplitude (m), t – time (s), T – period (s), x – position (m), λ – wavelength (m), φ₀ – initial phase (rad). For a wave travelling in the negative x direction, the sign of the spatial term changes.

3. Relations between the quantities

f = 1/T · v = λ/T = λf

3. Electric Current

1. Electric current and voltage

Electric current is the ordered motion of electrically charged particles. Its magnitude is the charge that passes through a cross-section of the conductor per unit time (for a constant current):

I = ΔQ/Δt [A]

The conventional direction of current in the external part of a circuit is from the positive terminal of the source to the negative one; in metals the electrons move in the opposite direction.

The voltage between two points expresses the work done by electric forces per unit charge: U = W/Q (with the appropriate definition of work between the points); the unit is the volt (V).

2. Electrical resistance and Ohm’s law

U = RI ⟺ R = U/I

The unit of resistance is the ohm (Ω); 1 Ω = 1 V/A. Ohm’s law in the form U = RI describes an ohmic conductor under constant physical conditions (especially temperature). The ratio U/I can also be calculated for a non-ohmic component at a given operating point, but it need not be constant.

3. Measuring resistance correctly

{{SVG2}}
  1. The ammeter is connected in series with the resistor so that the measured current flows through it.
  2. The voltmeter is connected in parallel with the resistor so that it measures the voltage across it.
  3. We measure the voltage U and the current I, then calculate R = U/I.

Illustrative measurement: If the voltmeter reads U = 6.0 V and the ammeter I = 0.030 A, then:

R = 6.0 / 0.030 = 200 Ω

The numerical values are illustrative; they serve to demonstrate the procedure.

4. What can affect the result

In a real measurement we take into account the internal resistance of the meters, the quality of the connections, the selected measuring range and possible heating of the resistor. In a school experiment we use a safe low-voltage source.
