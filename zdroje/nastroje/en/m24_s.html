1. Mechanical Waves

1.1 Formation of waves

A mechanical wave is a process in which oscillation spreads through an elastic medium. We need a source of oscillation and an elastic medium whose particles are bound to each other. An oscillating particle sets its neighbours into oscillation through these bonds, and each further particle starts to oscillate with a delay. A wave transfers energy, not matter – the particles oscillate about their equilibrium positions.

Transverse wave: the particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation (a wave on a rope or a string). It occurs in solids and on the surface of liquids.

Longitudinal wave: the particles oscillate along the direction of propagation, forming compressions and rarefactions (sound). It travels in solids, liquids and gases.

The wavelength λ is the distance the wave travels in one period. It is also the distance between the two nearest points oscillating in the same phase:

λ = vT = v/f

1.2 Equation of a progressive wave

If the source at x = 0 oscillates as y = ym sin ωt, a point at distance x oscillates with a delay of x/v:

y = ym sin 2π(t/T − x/λ)

y is the instantaneous displacement, ym the amplitude, T the period, f = 1/T the frequency, λ the wavelength and v the wave speed. The expression 2π(t/T − x/λ) is the phase of the wave.

1.3 Basic properties of waves

  • Reflection: at a fixed end the wave is reflected with the opposite phase, at a free end with the same phase.
  • Refraction: when passing into another medium the speed and direction of propagation change; the frequency stays the same.
  • Diffraction: the wave also spreads behind an obstacle, noticeably when the obstacle or opening is comparable with λ.
  • Interference: superposition of waves. The displacements add; the waves reinforce or weaken each other depending on the phase difference.

The propagation of waves is explained by Huygens’ principle: every point reached by the wave becomes a source of secondary wavelets.

1.4 Standing waves

A standing wave is formed by the interference of two waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions – typically an incident and a reflected wave.

  • Nodes do not oscillate at all; antinodes oscillate with the largest amplitude.
  • Adjacent nodes are λ/2 apart, a node and the adjacent antinode λ/4.
  • Points between two adjacent nodes oscillate in the same phase, points in neighbouring segments in opposite phase.
  • A standing wave does not transfer energy. For example, for a string fixed at both ends l = kλ/2.

2. Structure and Properties of Liquids – Capillarity

2.1 Surface layer and surface tension

A molecule inside a liquid is attracted equally in all directions by its neighbours. A molecule in the surface layer (about as thick as the range of molecular forces) has neighbours only below it, so the resultant force points into the liquid. The surface layer behaves like a stretched membrane and the liquid tends to reduce its surface area – that is why a small drop is spherical.

A surface force F acts on the edge of the surface layer, tangent to the surface and perpendicular to the edge. Surface tension is the ratio of this force to the length of the edge:

σ = F/l    [σ] = N/m

Surface tension depends on the kind of liquid and decreases with increasing temperature.

2.2 Wetting and capillarity

If the attractive forces between the molecules of the liquid and the wall are greater than those between the molecules of the liquid, the liquid wets the wall and forms a concave meniscus (water or petrol on glass). If they are smaller, the liquid does not wet the wall and the meniscus is convex (mercury on glass).

In a narrow tube – a capillary – a wetting liquid rises above the surrounding level (capillary rise), a non-wetting liquid falls below it (capillary depression). With complete wetting, the surface force around the circumference of the capillary balances the weight of the liquid column:

2πrσ = πr²hρg   →   h = 2σ/(rρg) = 4σ/(dρg)

Capillarity is important for the rise of water in soil and in plants, in a wick, and for the absorbency of paper and textiles.

3. Experiment – Coefficient of Sliding Friction

3.1 Theory

When a body slides on a surface, a friction force Ft opposes the motion. Its magnitude is directly proportional to the normal force FN with which the body presses on the surface:

Ft = μFN

μ is the coefficient of sliding friction. It depends on the material and finish of the surfaces in contact and is almost independent of the contact area and of the speed. On a horizontal surface with a horizontal pull FN = G = mg.

3.2 Equipment

A wooden block with a hook, a spring balance, a set of masses, scales, a horizontal board.

3.3 Arrangement

{{SVG2}}

3.4 Procedure

  1. Weigh the block and determine its weight G = mg.
  2. Hold the spring balance horizontally and pull the block at constant velocity in a straight line. Read the steady value of the force F.
  3. Repeat the measurement several times and take the mean.
  4. Add masses to the block and repeat the measurement for different normal forces.
  5. Calculate μ = F/FN and plot a graph of Ft against FN.

3.5 Why constant velocity

In uniform straight-line motion the resultant force is zero according to Newton’s first law. The pulling force of the spring balance is therefore equal to the friction force: F = Ft. If the block were accelerating, the pulling force would be greater than the friction force and the result would be too large.

The force needed to start the block moving is larger – it corresponds to static friction. That is why it is not used to determine sliding friction.

3.7 Uncertainties and another method

Errors are caused by uneven pulling, a spring balance held at an angle, an uneven board surface and reading a fluctuating pointer. Repeating the measurement and pulling slowly helps.

The coefficient can also be found on an inclined plane: we increase the slope of the board until the block, after a small push, slides at constant velocity. Then mg sin α = μmg cos α, so μ = tan α.
