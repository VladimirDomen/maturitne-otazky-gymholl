1. Magnetic Field

1.1 Magnetic field of a permanent magnet

A permanent magnet (made of steel, ferrite or neodymium alloys) produces a magnetic field around it. The field shows itself by forces on other magnets, ferromagnetic materials and current-carrying wires. A magnet has two poles – north N and south S. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel. The poles cannot be separated: breaking a magnet produces two smaller magnets, each with both poles.

The field is represented by magnetic field lines. Their tangent at a given point has the direction of the axis of a freely pivoted compass needle, and the line is oriented from the needle’s south pole to its north pole. Field lines are closed – outside the magnet they leave the north pole and enter the south pole, inside the magnet they continue from S to N. Where the lines are denser, the field is stronger.

{{SVG0}}

The force effect of the field is described by the vector quantity magnetic flux density B, measured in tesla (T). The Earth also has a magnetic field – its south magnetic pole is near the geographic North Pole, so the north pole of a compass needle points north.

1.2 Magnetic field of a current-carrying wire and a coil

A magnetic field forms around every current-carrying wire (Oersted’s experiment – the wire deflects a compass needle). The field lines of a straight wire are concentric circles in planes perpendicular to the wire. Their orientation is given by the right-hand grip rule: if the thumb points in the direction of the current, the fingers show the orientation of the field lines. The flux density at a distance d from a long straight wire:

B = μ₀ I / (2π d)     μ₀ = 4π · 10⁻⁷ T·m·A⁻¹

A current-carrying coil (solenoid) has a field similar to that of a bar magnet. Inside a long coil the field is uniform – the field lines are parallel:

B = μ₀ μᵣ N I / l

N is the number of turns, l the length of the coil and μᵣ the relative permeability of the core. The poles of the coil are found by the right-hand rule: fingers in the direction of the current in the turns, the thumb points to the north pole. A ferromagnetic core strengthens the field many times – an electromagnet.

1.3 Two parallel current-carrying wires

A force acts on a wire of length l carrying current I in a magnetic field of flux density B (its direction is given by Fleming’s left-hand rule):

F = B I l sin α

Each of two parallel wires lies in the magnetic field of the other, so they exert forces on each other of equal size and opposite direction (law of action and reaction):

F = μ₀ I₁ I₂ l / (2π d)
{{SVG2}}
  • currents in the same direction – the wires attract,
  • currents in opposite directions – the wires repel.

The force is proportional to the product of the currents and inversely proportional to the distance between the wires. The original definition of the ampere was based on this interaction.

1.4 Effect of a magnetic field on charged particles

A particle with charge Q moving with velocity v in a magnetic field experiences the Lorentz (magnetic) force:

Fm = Q v B sin α

α is the angle between the velocity and the flux density. The force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, so it changes only the direction of the velocity, not its magnitude – it does no work. A particle moving along the field lines experiences no force. If a particle enters a uniform field perpendicular to the field lines, it moves in a circle. The Lorentz force provides the centripetal force:

{{SVG3}}
Q v B = m v²/r    →    r = m v / (Q B)

Applications and occurrences: mass spectrometer (separating isotopes by the radius of the path), cyclotron and other accelerators, deflection of the electron beam in older picture tubes, Hall probe for measuring flux density. The Earth’s magnetic field deflects charged particles of the solar wind – near the poles this produces the aurora.

Summary: Magnetic fields are produced by magnets and moving charges (currents). The field acts on current-carrying wires with the force F = BIl sin α and on moving charged particles with the Lorentz force F = QvB sin α. Parallel wires with currents in the same direction attract, with opposite currents they repel.

2. Fluid Mechanics – Flow in a Pipe

2.1 Ideal liquid and flow

An ideal liquid is perfectly fluid (no internal friction) and incompressible. In steady flow the velocity at a given point does not change with time. A streamline is a line whose tangent at every point has the direction of the velocity of the moving particle. Streamlines do not cross.

2.2 Volume and mass flow rate, continuity equation

The volume flow rate is the volume of liquid flowing through a cross-section of the pipe per unit time. The mass flow rate is the mass of liquid flowing per unit time:

QV = V/t = S v  [m³·s⁻¹]     Qm = m/t = ρ S v  [kg·s⁻¹]

An ideal liquid does not accumulate or disappear anywhere, so the same volume passes through every cross-section of the pipe in the same time – the continuity equation:

S₁ v₁ = S₂ v₂ = const.

In a narrower place the liquid flows faster, in a wider place more slowly.

2.3 Bernoulli’s equation

The law of conservation of mechanical energy for a horizontal pipe implies that the sum of the kinetic energy per unit volume of the liquid and the pressure potential energy per unit volume is constant:

½ ρ v₁² + p₁ = ½ ρ v₂² + p₂

Where the liquid flows faster, its pressure is lower (the hydrodynamic paradox). This is used in the water-jet pump, sprayer, carburettor, in measuring flow speed (Venturi tube) and it explains the lift of an aircraft wing.

2.4 Solution of the task

Given: S₁ = 20 cm² = 2 · 10⁻³ m², v₁ = 8 m/s, S₂ = 40 cm² = 4 · 10⁻³ m², p₁ = 1.08 · 10⁵ Pa, ρ = 1 000 kg/m³.

{{SVG4}}

a) Volume flow rate:

QV = S₁ v₁ = 2 · 10⁻³ m² · 8 m/s = 1.6 · 10⁻² m³/s = 16 l/s

b) Mass flow rate:

Qm = ρ QV = 1 000 kg/m³ · 1.6 · 10⁻² m³/s = 16 kg/s

c) From the continuity equation:

v₂ = S₁ v₁ / S₂ = 20 cm² · 8 m/s / 40 cm² = 4 m/s

The cross-section doubled, so the speed halved. Both the volume and the mass flow rate in the wider part are the same as in the narrower part.

d) Extension task – from Bernoulli’s equation:

p₂ = p₁ + ½ ρ (v₁² − v₂²) = 1.08 · 10⁵ Pa + ½ · 1 000 · (8² − 4²) Pa
p₂ = 1.08 · 10⁵ Pa + 0.24 · 10⁵ Pa = 1.32 · 10⁵ Pa

In the wider part the water flows more slowly, so its pressure is higher.

QV = 1.6 · 10⁻² m³/s = 16 l/s; Qm = 16 kg/s; v₂ = 4 m/s; p₂ = 1.32 · 10⁵ Pa.

3. Experiment – Specific Latent Heat of Fusion of Ice

3.1 Theoretical background

The internal energy U of a body is the sum of the kinetic energies of the random motion of its particles and the potential energies of their mutual interaction. Internal energy can be changed in two ways:

  • by doing work W – e.g. by friction, by compressing a gas,
  • by heat transfer – a body at a higher temperature passes part of its internal energy to a body at a lower temperature. The energy transferred in this way is called heat Q.

Both ways are combined in the first law of thermodynamics: ΔU = W + Q. The heat needed to change the temperature of a body and the heat needed to melt a body at its melting point:

Q = m c Δt     Lt = m lt

The specific latent heat of fusion lt is the heat needed to melt 1 kg of a substance at its melting point [J·kg⁻¹]. During melting the temperature does not change; the energy supplied increases the potential energy of the particles (breaks down the crystal lattice).

In a thermally insulated system (a calorimeter) the calorimetric equation holds: the heat given out by the warmer bodies equals the heat taken in by the colder bodies.

3.2 Apparatus

Calorimeter with a stirrer (with a known heat capacity C, or determined beforehand), thermometer with 0.1 °C or 0.5 °C divisions, digital balance, warm water (about 30 °C), pieces of ice from a freezer left to warm to 0 °C (they start to melt), paper towel.

3.4 Procedure

  1. Weigh the empty inner vessel of the calorimeter, pour in water and weigh again – this gives the mass of water m₁.
  2. After stirring, measure the initial temperature of the water t₁.
  3. Dry the pieces of ice at 0 °C with a towel (water on the surface would distort the result) and put them into the calorimeter immediately. Cover the calorimeter.
  4. Stir and watch the temperature. When all the ice has melted, record the lowest steady temperature t.
  5. Weigh the calorimeter with the water again. The mass of ice m₂ follows from the difference in mass.
  6. Repeat the measurement at least twice and average the results.

3.5 Calorimetric equation and evaluation

The warm water and the calorimeter give out heat. The ice takes it in, first for melting (at 0 °C) and then the water from the ice for warming from 0 °C to t:

(m₁ c + C)(t₁ − t) = m₂ lt + m₂ c (t − 0 °C)
lt = [(m₁ c + C)(t₁ − t) − m₂ c t] / m₂

The following numbers are model data; they are not a real measurement. c = 4 180 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹.

lt = (15 570 − 2 170) J / 0.040 kg ≈ 3.35 · 10⁵ J/kg = 335 kJ/kg

The table value is 334 kJ/kg – the result agrees well.

3.6 Sources of error

  • Heat exchange with the surroundings – the calorimeter is not perfectly insulated. We therefore choose t₁ about as far above room temperature as t will be below it – the losses and gains partly cancel.
  • Water on the surface of the ice – it increases m₂ without using latent heat; the result would be too small.
  • Ice below 0 °C – part of the heat would be used to warm it; the result would be too large.
  • Accuracy of the thermometer and balance, neglecting the heat capacity of the thermometer and stirrer.
Conclusion: From the calorimetric equation (m₁c + C)(t₁ − t) = m₂lt + m₂ct the specific latent heat of fusion of ice comes out at about 335 kJ/kg (table value 334 kJ/kg).
