1. Alternating Current

1.1 Production of an alternating voltage

A loop (a coil with N turns) of area S rotates uniformly with angular velocity ω in a uniform magnetic field of flux density B. The magnetic flux through the loop changes periodically:

Φ = BS cos ωt

By Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction the voltage induced in the coil is u = −N·dΦ/dt:

u = Um sin ωt,    Um = NBSω,    ω = 2πf = 2π/T

An alternator works on this principle. The mains frequency is f = 50 Hz.

1.2 Instantaneous and rms values

The instantaneous values of voltage and current are harmonic functions of time:

u = Um sin ωt     i = Im sin(ωt + φ)

Um, Im are the amplitudes and φ is the phase difference of the current relative to the voltage. The graph is a sine curve with period T. The rms (effective) value is the value of a direct current that would produce the same heat in a resistor:

U = Um/√2     I = Im/√2

The mains voltage of 230 V is an rms value; the amplitude is about 325 V.

Effect on amplitude: for each component Im = Um/X. A coil opposes the current more at higher frequency – the induced voltage opposes the change of current. A capacitor, on the other hand, “passes” higher frequencies better, and a steady direct current does not flow through it.

1.4 Solution of the task

The coil has negligible resistance, so the current is limited only by the inductive reactance. The voltage and current are rms values:

XL = U/I = 230 V / 0.5 A = 460 Ω
L = XL/(2πf) = 460 Ω / (2π · 50 Hz) ≈ 1.46 H
The inductance of the coil is about 1.46 H.

A real coil also has winding resistance R. Then U/I = Z = √(R² + XL²) and the inductance would come out slightly smaller.

2. Molecular Physics and Thermodynamics – Calorimetric Equation

2.1 Internal energy and heat

The internal energy of a body is the sum of the kinetic energies of the random motion of its particles and the potential energies of their mutual interaction. Heat Q is the internal energy that a body gives or receives in thermal exchange – without doing work, only because of a temperature difference.

To change the temperature of a body by Δt we need heat:

Q = mcΔt = CΔt

c is the specific heat capacity of the substance [J/(kg·K)], C = mc the heat capacity of the body [J/K].

During melting the temperature does not change until the whole body has melted. The heat supplied increases the potential energy of the particles (breaks up the crystal lattice):

Lt = m · lt

2.2 Calorimetric equation

In a thermally isolated system the heat given by the warmer bodies equals the heat received by the colder bodies until the temperatures become equal:

Qgiven = Qreceived

It is the law of conservation of energy for thermal exchange. It holds if we neglect heat exchange with the surroundings and the system does no work.

2.3 Solution of the task

mw = 0.200 kg, tw = 80 °C, mi = 0.150 kg, C = 90 J/K, cw = 4 180 J/(kg·K), ci = 2 100 J/(kg·K), lt = 334 000 J/kg. The calorimeter was initially at the temperature of the water.

First we check whether all the ice melts. The hot water and the calorimeter can give, when cooling to 0 °C:

(mwcw + C) · 80 K = (836 + 90) J/K · 80 K = 74 080 J

Melting the ice at 0 °C needs milt = 50 100 J. For ice at −15 °C we need a further mici · 15 K = 4 725 J, in total 54 825 J. In both cases this is less than 74 080 J, so all the ice melts and the final temperature t is above 0 °C.

a) Ice at 0 °C. The water and the calorimeter cool from 80 °C to t. The ice melts and the resulting water warms from 0 °C to t:

(mwcw + C)(80 − t) = milt + micw(t − 0)
926(80 − t) = 50 100 + 627t
74 080 − 50 100 = 1 553t   →   t = 23 980/1 553 ≈ 15.4 °C

b) Ice at −15 °C. The ice must first warm to 0 °C:

(mwcw + C)(80 − t) = mici · 15 + milt + micwt
926(80 − t) = 4 725 + 50 100 + 627t
74 080 − 54 825 = 1 553t   →   t = 19 255/1 553 ≈ 12.4 °C
a) t ≈ 15.4 °C; b) t ≈ 12.4 °C. In both cases all the ice melts and the calorimeter contains 350 g of water.

The colder ice needs an extra 4 725 J to warm to 0 °C, so the final temperature is lower. Most of the heat is used by the melting itself – about 80 to 85 % of the heat the ice receives.

The heat capacity of the calorimeter belongs on the side of the bodies that give heat, because the calorimeter was initially at 80 °C.

3. Experiment – Resultant of Two Forces

3.1 Theory

Force is a vector. The resultant of two forces acting at one point is their vector sum. Graphically it is the diagonal of the parallelogram of forces constructed from F₁ and F₂. If the forces make an angle α, the magnitude of the resultant is:

F = √(F₁² + F₂² + 2F₁F₂ cos α)

The resultant is largest for α = 0° (F = F₁ + F₂) and smallest for α = 180° (F = |F₁ − F₂|). For α = 90° Pythagoras’ theorem applies.

{{SVG1}}

3.2 Equipment

Three spring balances, a small ring, threads, a board with paper, a protractor, a ruler.

3.3 Procedure

  1. Attach the ring with threads to three spring balances lying on the paper. Pull the balances in different directions until the ring is at rest.
  2. The ring is in equilibrium, so the third force F₃ has the same magnitude as the resultant of F₁ and F₂ but the opposite direction. F₃ is the equilibrant.
  3. Mark the directions of the threads on the paper and read the magnitudes F₁, F₂ and F₃. Measure the angle α between F₁ and F₂ with the protractor.
  4. Using a chosen scale (e.g. 1 N = 2 cm) construct the parallelogram of forces and measure the length of the diagonal.
  5. Also calculate the resultant from the formula for F and compare all three values. Repeat the measurement for other angles.

3.4 Sample evaluation

The following numbers are model data for F₁ = 3.0 N and F₂ = 4.0 N; they are not a real measurement.

For example, for α = 60°:

F = √(3² + 4² + 2 · 3 · 4 · cos 60°) N = √37 N ≈ 6.08 N

The direction of the resultant is given by the angle β it makes with F₁: tan β = F₂ sin α/(F₁ + F₂ cos α). For α = 60°, β ≈ 34.7°.

Conclusion: Within the accuracy of the spring balances, the measured equilibrant agrees with the resultant found graphically and by calculation. Forces add as vectors, not as numbers – as the angle α increases, the resultant decreases.

3.5 Uncertainties

Errors are caused by reading the spring balances, friction of the balances and the ring on the board, inaccurate marking of the thread directions and an inaccurate construction. Using balances with a suitable range and a larger drawing scale helps.
