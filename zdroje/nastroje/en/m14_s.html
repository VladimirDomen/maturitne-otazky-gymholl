1. Electric Current in Electrolytes

1.1 Electrolyte and electrolytic dissociation

An electrolyte is a solution or melt of a substance that breaks up into ions – acids, bases and salts. The splitting of molecules into ions caused by the solvent (e.g. water) is called electrolytic dissociation:

CuSO₄ → Cu²⁺ + SO₄²⁻

Ions can join again (recombination). The fraction of dissociated molecules is given by the degree of dissociation.

The charge carriers in an electrolyte are ions. When a voltage is applied, an electric field is set up in the electrolyte: positive cations move towards the cathode (negative electrode), negative anions towards the anode (positive electrode). The ions are deposited on the electrodes. Conduction is accompanied by transport of matter and chemical changes – electrolysis. Electrolytes are therefore called second-class conductors (metals are first-class conductors).

{{SVG0}}

In the electrolysis of CuSO₄ with copper electrodes:

cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu     anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻

Copper builds up on the cathode and the copper anode dissolves at the same rate. The concentration of the solution does not change.

1.2 Current–voltage characteristic

{{SVG1}}

Electrodes do not change (e.g. copper electrodes in CuSO₄ solution): the characteristic is a straight line through the origin and the electrolyte obeys Ohm’s law I = U/R.

Inert electrodes (e.g. platinum or carbon in dilute acid): the products of electrolysis settle on the electrodes and form a galvanic cell with the electrolyte. Its voltage acts against the external voltage (polarisation of the electrodes). A significant current starts to flow only when the decomposition voltage Ur is exceeded; then approximately:

I = (U − Ur)/R

For the electrolysis of water the decomposition voltage is about 1.7 V. The resistance of an electrolyte decreases as the temperature rises – more ions are dissociated and the viscosity of the solution decreases.

1.3 Faraday’s laws

Faraday’s first law: the mass of a substance deposited on an electrode is directly proportional to the charge that has passed through the electrolyte:

m = A · Q = A · I · t

Faraday’s second law: the electrochemical equivalent of a substance is directly proportional to its molar mass and inversely proportional to the number of elementary charges of the ion:

A = M/(F · z)    →    m = M · I · t/(F · z)

1.4 Technical applications

  • Electroplating – nickel, chromium or gold plating; the object is the cathode; protection against corrosion and appearance.
  • Electroforming – metal copies of objects and moulds.
  • Electrolytic refining – production of very pure copper (impure copper is the anode, pure copper is deposited on the cathode).
  • Production of metals and substances – aluminium by electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide, sodium, chlorine and sodium hydroxide, hydrogen by electrolysis of water.
  • Anodising – anodic oxidation of aluminium, protective layer.
  • Accumulators – charging is electrolysis; also electrolytic capacitors and electropolishing.

1.5 Solution of the task

Given: I = 3 A, m = 1.778 g = 1.778 · 10⁻³ kg, A = 0.33 · 10⁻⁶ kg·C⁻¹.

a) From Faraday’s first law m = AQ:

Q = m/A = 1.778 · 10⁻³ kg / (0.33 · 10⁻⁶ kg·C⁻¹) ≈ 5 390 C

b) For a constant current Q = It:

t = Q/I = m/(A · I) = 1.778 · 10⁻³ / (0.33 · 10⁻⁶ · 3) s ≈ 1 800 s = 30 min

c) Copper is present in the solution as Cu²⁺ ions, so z = 2:

A = M/(F · z) = 63.5 · 10⁻³ kg·mol⁻¹ / (96 485 C·mol⁻¹ · 2) ≈ 0.329 · 10⁻⁶ kg·C⁻¹

The value given in the task is correctly rounded.

d) Amount of deposited copper and the number of atoms:

n = m/M = 1.778 g / 63.5 g·mol⁻¹ = 0.028 mol
N = n · NA = 0.028 mol · 6.022 · 10²³ mol⁻¹ ≈ 1.69 · 10²² atoms

Each Cu²⁺ ion needs two electrons at the cathode. The number of electrons also follows from the charge:

Ne = Q/e = 5 390 C / 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 3.36 · 10²² ≈ 2N
Q ≈ 5.4 · 10³ C, t ≈ 1 800 s = 30 min; about 1.7 · 10²² copper atoms were deposited and about 3.4 · 10²² electrons passed.

2. Energy of a Particle

2.1 Kinetic and potential energy

Kinetic energy is the energy a body has because of its motion:

Ek = ½ m v²    [E] = J

Gravitational potential energy is the energy a body has because of its position in the gravitational field. In a uniform field near the Earth’s surface:

Ep = m g h

The height h is measured from a chosen zero level, so the potential energy depends on this choice – only its changes have physical meaning. An elastically deformed body (a spring) also has potential energy: Ep = ½ k y².

2.2 Energy and work

The work done by the resultant force equals the change in the kinetic energy of the body:

W = ΔEk = ½ m v₂² − ½ m v₁²

When a body descends by a height Δh, the gravitational force does work W = mgΔh and the potential energy decreases by the same amount: W = −ΔEp. When we lift a body, an external force does work and the potential energy increases.

2.3 Law of conservation of mechanical energy

The sum of kinetic and potential energy is the mechanical energy E = Ek + Ep.

Law of conservation of mechanical energy: in an isolated system in which only conservative forces act (gravitational, elastic), the mechanical energy is constant:

Ek + Ep = const.    →    ΔEk = −ΔEp

Energy only changes from one form into another. If friction or the resistance of the medium acts, part of the mechanical energy is converted into the internal energy of the bodies and the surroundings. The total energy is always conserved (law of conservation of energy).

2.4 Solution of the task

Given: m = 1 kg, h = 45 m, v₀ = 0, g = 10 m/s². The body moves with uniform acceleration g.

a) Time t = 0 s: the body is at rest at a height of 45 m.

Ek0 = 0 J     Ep0 = m g h = 1 · 10 · 45 J = 450 J

b) During the first second the body travels a distance:

s = ½ g t² = ½ · 10 · 1² m = 5 m   →   h₁ = h − s = 45 m − 5 m = 40 m
v₁ = g t = 10 m/s² · 1 s = 10 m/s

c) Energies at time t = 1 s:

Ek1 = ½ m v₁² = ½ · 1 · 10² J = 50 J     Ep1 = m g h₁ = 1 · 10 · 40 J = 400 J

d) Changes in energy during the first second:

ΔEk = 50 J − 0 J = +50 J     ΔEp = 400 J − 450 J = −50 J

The kinetic energy increased by exactly as much as the potential energy decreased. The work done by gravity has the same value: W = mgs = 1 · 10 · 5 J = 50 J.

e) The mechanical energy is the same at both instants:

E = Ek0 + Ep0 = 0 J + 450 J = 450 J     E = Ek1 + Ep1 = 50 J + 400 J = 450 J

At impact (h = 0) all the energy is kinetic:

½ m v² = m g h   →   v = √(2gh) = √(2 · 10 · 45) m/s = 30 m/s

3. Experiment – Operation and Efficiency of a Transformer

3.1 Theory

A transformer changes an alternating voltage. It has two coils on a common closed core made of ferromagnetic sheets. The alternating current in the primary coil (N₁ turns) produces a varying magnetic flux in the core. This flux induces an alternating voltage of the same frequency in the secondary coil (N₂ turns). For an ideal transformer:

U₂/U₁ = N₂/N₁ = k     I₂/I₁ ≈ N₁/N₂

The number k is the turns ratio. For N₂ > N₁ the transformer steps the voltage up (and the current down), for N₂ < N₁ it steps the voltage down. A transformer works only with alternating current – a direct current does not produce a varying magnetic flux and no voltage is induced in the secondary coil.

Efficiency is the ratio of the output power delivered to the load (P₂) to the input power taken from the source (P₁):

η = P₂/P₁ = (U₂ I₂)/(U₁ I₁) · 100 %

This relation holds when the load is resistive (a bulb, a resistor) and the transformer is sufficiently loaded. Then voltage and current are approximately in phase. Exactly, the input power is P₁ = U₁I₁ cos φ₁.

Energy losses (P₁ − P₂ is converted into internal energy):

  • copper losses – Joule heating P = RI² in the resistance of the coils; reduced by thicker copper wire,
  • core losses – eddy currents (reduced by a core of thin, mutually insulated sheets) and repeated magnetisation, hysteresis (reduced by a soft ferromagnetic material),
  • flux leakage – part of the flux passes outside the secondary coil; reduced by a closed core without gaps.

Large transformers in the power grid have an efficiency of 98–99 %, small school transformers usually 70–90 %.

3.2 Apparatus

Demountable school transformer (e.g. coils with 600 and 300 turns, U-shaped core with a yoke), source of safe AC voltage (e.g. 12 V, 50 Hz), two ammeters and two voltmeters on AC ranges (or two wattmeters), load – resistors or a rheostat (e.g. 5–20 Ω, rated for several watts), leads.

3.3 Circuit

{{SVG4}}

In the primary circuit ammeter A₁ measures current I₁ and voltmeter V₁ the voltage U₁ across the primary coil. In the secondary circuit A₂ measures the current I₂ through the load and V₂ the voltage U₂ across the secondary coil.

3.4 Verifying the operation – measurement without load

  1. Assemble the transformer with a closed core (the yoke fits well) and connect the circuit according to the diagram, for now without a load. Record N₁ and N₂.
  2. Apply an alternating voltage U₁ to the primary coil and read U₁ and U₂.
  3. Replace the secondary coil with coils of a different number of turns and repeat the measurement.
  4. For each pair compare U₂/U₁ with N₂/N₁.
  5. For comparison apply a DC voltage to the primary – voltmeter V₂ reads zero (it deflects briefly only when switching on and off).

3.5 Extension – measuring the efficiency

The efficiency can be checked at school with the same meters, but the result is only approximate (see below).

  1. With N₁ = 600 and N₂ = 300 connect a load R to the secondary and read U₁, I₁, U₂, I₂. Repeat the measurement for several different loads.
  2. For each measurement calculate P₁ = U₁I₁, P₂ = U₂I₂ and η = P₂/P₁.
  3. For comparison the yoke can be removed – the flux leakage increases, U₂ drops and the efficiency decreases considerably.

For example, for R = 10 Ω:

η = (5.6 V · 0.56 A)/(12.0 V · 0.33 A) = 3.14 W / 3.96 W ≈ 0.79 = 79 %

At a small load a large part of the primary current is the magnetising current, which carries only reactive power. The product U₁I₁ is then greater than the real input power and the calculated efficiency comes out lower. At a large current the copper losses (RI²) increase and the voltage U₂ drops. The efficiency is therefore greatest at a medium load.

3.6 Sources of error and safety

Work only with a safe low voltage – never connect the primary coil directly to the 230 V mains. Change the circuit only with the source switched off. The load must withstand the given power. Errors are caused by the tolerances of the meters on AC ranges, neglecting the phase shift (cos φ₁) and the consumption of the meters themselves. More accurate results are obtained by measuring input and output power with wattmeters.

Conclusion: The ratio of the voltages across the coils equals the ratio of their numbers of turns, U₂/U₁ ≈ N₂/N₁ – the transformer works on the principle of electromagnetic induction and only with alternating current. The efficiency found from η = U₂I₂/(U₁I₁) is about 70–80 % for a school transformer with a resistive load; losses occur in the windings, in the core and through flux leakage.
